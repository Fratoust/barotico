Benin

Presidential republic · about 14 million people · a parliament with no opposition since February 2026

Next vote≈ 74 monthsNext general elections expected in 2033 (legislative and local in January, presidential in April; no date set)
The November 2025 constitutional revision gave the president, deputies, mayors and local councillors seven-year terms. The deputies and councillors elected on 11 January 2026 and the president elected on 12 April 2026 are therefore due for re-election in 2033 (month estimated from the 2026 calendar). Deputies voted in July 2026 to suspend the electoral commission (CENA) 'provisionally', citing the absence of major elections for six years; the body that will run the 2033 vote has not yet been defined. source

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. On the posterContinuity: 'consolidate the gains' of the reforms, starting with the 2019 constitutional revision
⚠ Rights alertIts 36 deputies and the 47 of the Union progressiste, the only two parties in the parliament elected in April 2019 without the opposition, adopted the revision unanimously on 1 November 2019. On 27 November and 4 December 2020 (XYZ, Houngue Éric Noudehouenou and Ajavon v. Benin) the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights ruled that it breached the duty to base constitutional change on national consensus (art. 10(2) of the African Charter on Democracy, Elections and Governance) and ordered the repeal of Law 2019-40 and of the 2019 electoral code before any election. The government it sat in contests the ruling. source · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 · 6
On the posterAppeasement after the 2019 post-election crisis: an amnesty recommended by the October 2019 political dialogue
⚠ Rights alertOn 31 October 2019 its deputies and those of the Union progressiste unanimously adopted Law 2019-39, an amnesty for all offences committed around the April 2019 legislative elections: prosecutions ended and detainees were freed. On 4 December 2020 the African Court (Ajavon v. Benin), which found that security forces' live fire during the May 2019 protests violated the rights to life and to physical integrity, ruled that the amnesty denied victims their right to have their cause heard (art. 7(1) of the African Charter) and ordered its repeal and investigations. No position of the party on this part of the ruling was found. source · 2 · 3
On the posterEducation for young people: extend school canteens (coalition government, Government Action Programme)
DeliveredThe national school feeding programme went from 2,199 schools and 425,231 pupils (about 31% coverage) in 2017-18 to 5,532 schools and 1,025,203 pupils (75%) at the end of 2021-22; in canteen schools, girls rose from 282,751 in 2019 to 326,793 in 2021 (+15.5%), according to a 2024 case study for the Research Consortium for School Health and Nutrition. source
Also worth knowing
  • In June 2025 its own Energy and Water minister, Paulin Akponna, said in Parakou that tens of billions of FCFA meant for electricity and drinking water had been diverted. He was dismissed on 26 June, the BR expressed its 'entire disapproval', and he then apologised, saying no evidence supported his remarks. The anti-corruption authority (HCPC) found no proof of embezzlement in August 2025; a parliamentary inquiry was opened in July. source
  • Its leader called the 2026 elections decisive for 'the future of our democracy'; under the 2024 electoral code passed by the majority it belongs to, the BR and the UPR now hold all 109 seats, and the opposition's 16.20% earned none. source
  • It promises 'public services that better meet people's needs'; its group backed the 2025 revision that let deputies sit seven years instead of five between two votes. source
To be fair
  • Nominated Mariam Chabi Talata as running mate, making her the vice-president of the 2026-2033 term
  • Member of the governments under which the deficit stayed at the 3% UEMOA ceiling and debt fell to 53.4% of GDP in 2024
  • Its coalition's governments extended school meals to 1.16 million pupils by 2022

3 to 4 concrete promises from its latest programme, each checked against an official act or an independent tracker. “Made a difference” needs an independent measure of the effect on people; a promise adopted without measurable effect, symbolic or quickly reversed counts as “on paper” (a quarter of a point). “Partly” also covers a promise the party pushed but others blocked. A promise that needed power the party never held cannot be tested.

If you're considering voting for Bloc républicain, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

It is the junior partner of a majority that holds every seat in parliament. Its leader worries about the future of democracy.

In 2019 its deputies adopted a new constitution unanimously, in a parliament without opposition. In 2020 the African Court ordered its repeal, for lack of national consensus.

When its own minister said tens of billions had been diverted, the party disapproved of the minister. He apologised; the anti-corruption authority found no proof of embezzlement.

To be fair, the governments it sat in took school meals to 1.16 million pupils, with girls' numbers in those schools up 15.5%.
