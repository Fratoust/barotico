Benin

Presidential republic · about 14 million people · a parliament with no opposition since February 2026

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

The November 2025 constitutional revision gave the president, deputies, mayors and local councillors seven-year terms. The deputies and councillors elected on 11 January 2026 and the president elected on 12 April 2026 are therefore due for re-election in 2033 (month estimated from the 2026 calendar). 109 votes out of 109: the Speaker of the National Assembly was elected unanimously in February 2026.

Joseph Djogbénou, president of the UPR, was the only candidate on 8 February 2026: every deputy belongs to the UPR or the Bloc républicain, because Les Démocrates' 16.20% in January fell short of the 20% needed to get any seat. Seventy-four days after leaving the presidency, Patrice Talon was elected president of the new, unelected Senate by 24 votes out of 25, with one blank ballot.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

There is no separate registration step: the electoral roll is drawn from the ANIP population register (RAVIP). Check that your details and phone number are up to date with ANIP (town hall, eservices.anip.bj, the ANIP BJ app or USSD 1511#) and bring your national ID card ('carte c'est moi') on polling day. Polling stations are also set up for the diaspora. The next national vote is expected in 2033, and the body that will run it is yet to be created.

Last turnout: 63.57% in the presidential election of 12 April 2026, after 36.74% in the legislative and local elections of 11 January 2026 source

Who decides what

President and vice-presidentElected together on one ticket by direct vote, now for seven years, renewable once, with a lifetime limit of two terms. The president heads both the state and the government (there is no prime minister) and appoints the ministers. Since 24 May 2026: Romuald Wadagni, with Vice-President Mariam Chabi Talata.
National Assembly109 deputies elected for seven years in 24 constituencies; it votes laws and the budget and can revise the Constitution with a four-fifths majority. A deputy who leaves the party that nominated him loses his seat. Since February 2026 all 109 seats belong to the UPR (60) and the Bloc républicain (49). Speaker: Joseph Djogbénou.
SenateCreated by the 2025 revision and installed on 30 July 2026: 25 members, none elected, made up of former heads of state by right (Talon, Soglo, Boni Yayi) and designated figures such as former heads of institutions. It receives every law at the same time as the president, 'regulates political life' and can suspend or withdraw the political or civic rights of some actors. President since 6 August 2026: Patrice Talon.
CourtsThe Constitutional Court validates candidacies, proclaims election results and reviews laws (it upheld the rejection of Les Démocrates' presidential ticket in October 2025). The CRIET, a special court created in 2016 for economic crimes and terrorism, has tried most high-profile political cases, including opponents and former allies of the government.
Communes77 communes with councils elected on the same day as the deputies, now for seven years; mayors and deputies elected together are the only officials who can sponsor a presidential candidate.

How people vote

  • About 7.9 million voters were expected in 2026. The electoral roll is built from the biometric population census (RAVIP) run by the identification agency ANIP; voters identify themselves with the national ID card ('carte c'est moi'). Polling stations also open abroad.
  • The 109 deputies are elected by party-list proportional representation in 24 constituencies, but since the March 2024 electoral code a party needs 20% of the national vote (up from 10%) to get any seat; in 2026 only two parties passed it.
  • A presidential ticket needs the sponsorship of 15% of deputies and mayors (28 elected officials), who may only sponsor their own party's candidate. The president is elected in up to two rounds; Wadagni won in the first round in 2026.

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from the government (adopted in the Council of Ministers) or from deputies; the 2025 constitutional revision was a proposal by majority deputies.
  2. The National Assembly examines and votes the text; a constitutional revision needs four fifths of deputies (it passed by 90 votes to 19 in November 2025).
  3. Since 2026 the adopted law is sent at the same time to the president and to the president of the Senate.
  4. The Constitutional Court can review its conformity; the president promulgates it.

Why it's complicated

Benin, long cited as a West African model of alternation, now has a parliament without opposition, an unelected Senate chaired by the former president, and no national election until 2033. The main opposition party, Les Démocrates, won 16.20% in January 2026 and no seat under the 20% threshold; its presidential ticket was rejected for lack of sponsorships after one of its own deputies withdrew his. The only opposition candidate, Paul Hounkpè of the FCBE, took 5.73%; his party joined the presidential majority a month later. A coup attempt on 7 December 2025, foiled with help from Nigeria and France, led to dozens of detentions, including an LD vice-president. Freedom House rates Benin Partly Free (61/100), scores judicial independence 2 of 4 and writes that Talon 'began using the justice system to attack his political opponents' after 2016; RSF dropped Benin 21 places in 2026. The economy, meanwhile, grew 8.1% in 2025 (INStaD).

Why it's like thisBenin's 1990 National Conference ended a single-party military regime and gave the country a constitution with a five-year presidential term limited to two mandates; power then changed hands peacefully at several elections. Patrice Talon, elected in 2016 promising a single term, reorganised the party system: dozens of parties merged into the two large blocs of the presidential camp, the UPR and the Bloc républicain, while successive electoral codes raised the thresholds for seats and candidacies. The 2025 revision kept the two-term limit but lengthened all terms to seven years and added a Senate of former leaders, which its supporters present as a guarantor of stability and its critics as a way for the outgoing camp to keep control.

Who's at the wheel

President Romuald Wadagni (finance minister under Talon, sworn in on 24 May 2026) and Vice-President Mariam Chabi Talata, with 22 ministers and 3 ministers delegate named the same day: Aristide Médenou (Economy and Finance), Yvon Détchénou (Justice), Djibril Mama Cissé Moussa (Interior, minister delegate), Corinne Amori Brunet (Foreign Affairs), Gildas Agonkan (Defence, minister delegate). Backed by the UPR and the Bloc républicain, joined by the FCBE.

Elected on 12 April 2026 in the first round with 94.27% of valid votes against 5.73% for Paul Hounkpè, on a 63.57% turnout (Constitutional Court, provisional results, confirmed on 23 April with no challenge filed). The main opposition party had no candidate.
