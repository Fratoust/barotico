Country profile · 1 Nov 2026

Ukraine

Unitary semi-presidential republic · under martial law since February 2022; no census since 2001, so no reliable population figure

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Next vote— No election date: Ukrainian law bars presidential, parliamentary and local elections while martial law lasts. It was extended for the 21st time on 26 October 2026, until 29 January 2027.
The Rada was last elected in July 2019 and President Zelensky in April 2019; his five-year term formally ended in May 2024. Article 19 of the martial-law act bans elections, and Article 108 of the Constitution says the President keeps his powers until a newly elected President takes office. In February 2025 the Rada backed this 'continuity of power' by 268 votes. A former Constitutional Court judge has said the martial-law act settles the question for now, while having doubts about some of its provisions; the Constitutional Court has not ruled. In May 2026 a Rada working group reaffirmed that no vote is possible under martial law. On 11 October 2026, after Donald Trump said Ukraine should elect a new leader, Zelensky said Ukraine would organise elections if Russia agreed to a full 60-day ceasefire. No ceasefire is in force; a US-Ukraine-Russia meeting was expected in early November. source

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Citizens from 18 are included automatically in the State Register of Voters at their registered address. You can ask the register office to change your voting address before an election, and citizens abroad vote at embassy and consulate polling stations. No election can be held until martial law ends; millions of displaced people and refugees abroad are one of the practical problems the Rada's working group on post-war elections is studying.

Last turnout: 49.84% in the July 2019 parliamentary snap election, the lowest ever for a Rada vote (Central Election Commission) source

Who decides what

PresidentHead of state and commander-in-chief, elected directly for five years (two terms at most). Volodymyr Zelensky won the April 2019 run-off with about 73%. He leads foreign and defence policy, chairs the National Security and Defence Council (NSDC) and proposes the Foreign and Defence Ministers.
Cabinet of MinistersAppointed by and answerable to the Rada. Serhiy Koretsky, former head of Naftogaz, became Prime Minister on 16 July 2026 (289 votes), two days after the Rada dismissed Yulia Svyrydenko's cabinet.
Verkhovna RadaSingle chamber of 450 seats, elected for five years; it passes laws and the budget and appoints the government. With no by-elections possible under martial law, 392 MPs were sitting in October 2026.
Courts and anti-corruption bodiesThe Constitutional Court and the Supreme Court sit at the top. Corruption by senior officials is investigated by NABU, prosecuted by SAPO and judged by the High Anti-Corruption Court (HACC), all created after 2014.
Regions and communities24 oblasts, the city of Kyiv, the Crimean autonomy and Sevastopol (both occupied since 2014). Since 2020 local government rests on merged communities (hromadas); under martial law military administrations run frontline areas.

How people vote

  • Voting is voluntary from 18. No national or local election can be called while martial law is in force.
  • The President is elected by direct vote, with a run-off between the top two if no one wins more than half.
  • The 2019 Rada was elected half by national party lists (5% threshold) and half in single-member districts; only 424 seats could be filled because of the occupied areas. The 2020 Electoral Code replaces this with proportional open regional lists for the next vote.

How a law is passed

  1. Bills are tabled by the President, MPs or the Cabinet of Ministers.
  2. The Rada usually votes in two readings and needs 226 votes; amendments added at second reading can change a bill completely, as with law 12414 on NABU in July 2025.
  3. The President signs or vetoes within 15 days; a veto is overridden by 300 votes.
  4. The Constitution needs 300 votes to change and cannot be amended under martial law; the Constitutional Court can strike down laws.

Why it's complicated

Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022 brought martial law, extended 21 times so far. Elections are banned while it lasts: MPs elected in 2019 serve without a fresh mandate and President Zelensky, whose term formally ended in May 2024, stays in office under Article 108 of the Constitution until a successor is elected (Freedom House: 51/100, Partly Free). Zelensky said in February 2026 that 90% of Ukrainians oppose a vote without a ceasefire, and in October 2026 Zelensky offered elections in exchange for a 60-day ceasefire that Russia has not granted. Meanwhile corruption cases test the system: in July 2025 the Rada voted to put the anti-corruption bureau NABU and prosecutor SAPO under the Prosecutor General, then reversed itself nine days later after street protests and EU warnings. NABU's 'Midas' energy case reached a former energy and justice minister and a co-owner of Kvartal 95, the studio Zelensky co-founded; the President's chief of staff Andriy Yermak resigned after a search in November 2025 and was notified of a money-laundering suspicion in May 2026. Five MPs of the ruling party were charged with taking money for their votes and, in a separate case, opposition leader Yulia Tymoshenko with offering undue benefits to MPs (presumption of innocence applies), and the former President Petro Poroshenko is under NSDC sanctions upheld by the Supreme Court in July 2026. The government itself changed in July 2026.

Why it's like thisUkraine's 1996 Constitution created a strong presidency. After the 2004 Orange Revolution, a reform shifted powers to parliament; the Constitutional Court reversed it in 2010 under President Viktor Yanukovych, and after the 2014 Maidan protests the Rada restored the 2004 version. The aim since then has been to balance a directly elected President against a Rada that appoints the government. The anti-corruption bodies (NABU, SAPO, HACC) and electronic asset declarations were created after 2014, partly as conditions for visa-free travel, IMF aid and, since 2022, EU candidate status.

Who's at the wheel

President Volodymyr Zelensky and Prime Minister Serhiy Koretsky's government (since 16 July 2026), backed by Servant of the People, which holds 229 of the 392 sitting seats, with frequent extra votes from the Dovira group and others.

Zelensky won the 2019 presidential run-off with about 73%; his party then took 43.16% of list votes and an outright majority in the July 2019 snap election. No vote has been possible since 2022. The Rada dismissed Yulia Svyrydenko's cabinet on 14 July 2026 (258 votes) and appointed Koretsky two days later (289 votes).

Its lead author is Holos MP Roman Lozynskyi; Servant of the People MPs, including party chair Olena Shuliak, co-signed it.In a joint opinion adopted in October 2023 (CDL-AD(2023)025) on base bill 9081, which bars from elections people who were members of banned parties at the start of the full-scale invasion, the Venice Commission and OSCE/ODIHR found that restrictions applied automatically, on party membership and elected office alone, without distinguishing members who took part in unlawful activities, impose collective responsibility and "contradict the principle of proportionality and may lead to arbitrary decisions" (right to stand for election). They called the 10-year period too long and asked for individual assessment and judicial review, while accepting that some restrictions after martial law may pursue a legitimate aim. Bill 9081-1 contains the same automatic restriction, with a wider scope. In February 2026 Lozynskyi said a direct ban on standing is complex and debatable under international standards, and proposed as a minimum step marking on the ballot candidates who led parties banned by court after 2014 ("soft lustration"); the bill has not been withdrawn. source · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 · 6Guarantee at stake: free elections and pluralism
