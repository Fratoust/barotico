Ukraine

Unitary semi-presidential republic · under martial law since February 2022; no census since 2001, so no reliable population figure

Before you read
  • The approach: we set what parties say against what they do, and every fact links to an official or independent source.
  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.
  • Dates: the date at the top is the profile's last edit. Each section gives its own source and, where it applies, the date of the data or of the last check; in “Watch list”, some dates are upcoming deadlines.
Next vote— No election date: Ukrainian law bars presidential, parliamentary and local elections while martial law lasts. It was extended for the 21st time on 26 October 2026, until 29 January 2027.
The Rada was last elected in July 2019 and President Zelensky in April 2019; his five-year term formally ended in May 2024. Article 19 of the martial-law act bans elections, and Article 108 of the Constitution says the President keeps his powers until a newly elected President takes office. In February 2025 the Rada backed this 'continuity of power' by 268 votes. A former Constitutional Court judge has said the martial-law act settles the question for now, while having doubts about some of its provisions; the Constitutional Court has not ruled. In May 2026 a Rada working group reaffirmed that no vote is possible under martial law. On 11 October 2026, after Donald Trump said Ukraine should elect a new leader, Zelensky said Ukraine would organise elections if Russia agreed to a full 60-day ceasefire. No ceasefire is in force; a US-Ukraine-Russia meeting was expected in early November. source

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

How to read the profiles

Positions on the left-right axis come from the Chapel Hill Expert Survey (or the closest expert survey where it does not cover the country). Profiles are ordered by number of seats. The judicial record only lists cases with an official act (ruling, charge, sanction, expulsion), with the same caps for every party; relatives and non-members are left out. The number of cases partly reflects a party's size and years in power.

No national vote has been held since 2019, so the parties' latest programmes date from 2019, and four of the eight parliamentary groups (Platform for Life and Peace, Dovira, Restoration of Ukraine and, in practice, For the Future) have no current programme of their own; their promises are taken from their 2019 lists or their leaders' public statements, and programme links are left empty. Seats are the Rada's own count in October 2026. Positions on the left-right axis are editorial estimates: we could not verify Chapel Hill Expert Survey scores for Ukraine (see report).

On 27 July Yulia Tymoshenko said the bodies must be truly independent of the President, the government and the Prosecutor General, and tabled an alternative in which an assembly of Heroes of Ukraine would choose their leaders. Also worth knowing
  • Says it fights corruption; 15 of its MPs voted for law 12414 curbing NABU on 22 July 2025. Yulia Tymoshenko then said the anti-corruption bodies must be independent of everyone and backed her own alternative, and only 4 of its MPs voted for the law restoring their independence nine days later. source
  • Calls itself a party of ordinary people; in 2025 its central office lived on its share of more than UAH 760 million of state funding, with private fundraising CHESNO describes as no better than the ruling party's UAH 1,000. source
  • Says it stands by the army; Yulia Tymoshenko called the government's mobilisation bill 'a harsh and cynical plan' and asked for demobilisation after 18 months of service; 2 of its MPs voted for the April 2024 law. source
To be fair
  • Has had a group in every Rada since 1999, one of the longest continuous records among Ukrainian parties
  • Its long campaign for cheaper household gas matches what wartime governments have done: in October 2025 the government kept household prices frozen through the heating season (UAH 7.96 per cubic metre for Naftogaz customers)
  • Opposed opening the farmland market without a referendum (2020), in line with its programme

A promise that needed power the party never held cannot be tested.

If you're considering voting for Batkivshchyna, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

It campaigns for clean politics. In July 2025, 15 of its MPs voted to place the anti-corruption bureau under the Prosecutor General.

Yulia Tymoshenko then said the bureau must be independent of everyone, and backed her own bill, in which Heroes of Ukraine would pick its leaders. When the law restoring its independence came to the vote, 4 of its MPs voted for it.

Its signature promises have not passed: its bills to cut gas and heating prices stalled, and the farmland market opened in 2020 without a single one of its votes.

To be fair, wartime governments have done part of what it asked: household gas has stayed frozen, at UAH 7.96 per cubic metre at Naftogaz through the 2025-26 heating season.
