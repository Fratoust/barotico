Russia

Federal semi-presidential republic · about 146 million people (Rosstat count, which includes Crimea)

Before you read
  • The approach: we set what parties say against what they do, and every fact links to an official or independent source.
  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.
  • Dates: the date at the top is the profile's last edit. Each section gives its own source and, where it applies, the date of the data or of the last check; in “Watch list”, some dates are upcoming deadlines.
Next vote≈ 40 monthsNext national vote: presidential election, due in March 2030. Elections are held but are not free (Freedom House: 'Not Free'; no OSCE observation in 2026)
The State Duma was elected on 18-20 September 2026; the next one is due in 2031. Vladimir Putin's current term runs to 2030, and a 2021 law resetting his term count allows him to run again in 2030 and to stay in office until 2036. The 2024 presidential vote was held in mid-March, so the countdown uses March 2030 as an estimate. The OSCE's election office (ODIHR) did not observe the 2026 vote; the Foreign Ministry said it had 'established itself as an instrument of double standards'. source
How to read this country profile

The profile has seven tabs. Start with the system to understand the rules of the game, then look at who governs and what the parties do. Every piece of information links to its source: click to check.

  1. How the country is run: institutions, elections, who passes the laws, and the political and economic context of the moment.
  2. Who is in power and how they got there, what they promised, what independent promise trackers say, and the measured effects of their action.
  3. One card per party, opened with a click: its place on the left-right axis, what it says set against what it does, its promises one by one and its democracy barometer.
  4. How to vote in practice, the electoral system, turnout, and what a blank vote changes.
  5. The country's indicators (life expectancy, schooling, homicides, press freedom, freedoms, corruption), each with its source.
  6. Upcoming deadlines and pending cases, with their exact status on the update date.
  7. The country's main media, who owns them, and what to know about its news landscape.

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

The same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico does not recommend any vote.

How to read the profiles

Positions on the left-right axis come from the Chapel Hill Expert Survey (or the closest expert survey where it does not cover the country). Profiles are ordered by number of seats. The judicial record only lists cases with an official act (ruling, charge, sanction, expulsion), with the same caps for every party; relatives and non-members are left out. The number of cases partly reflects a party's size and years in power.

Programme links are left empty: we did not open the parties' own programme pages. Stated values and promises come from party congresses and programmes as reported by the Russian press (state and independent). Positions on the left-right axis are editorial estimates (see each card).

On the posterReverse the 2018 pension reform
ConsistentIts deputies voted against raising the pension age to 60 for women and 65 for men, with the rest of the parliamentary opposition; the reform passed 332 to 83 on 27 September 2018, backed by United Russia. Its August 2021 bill to restore the old ages, like A Just Russia's, was rejected in December 2021; United Russia's Svetlana Bessarab said the labour committee proposed rejecting both. source · 2 · 3
On the posterFair elections and a real opposition
ConsistentOn 10 March 2020 its faction said it would vote against the amendment resetting Vladimir Putin's presidential terms, which passed 380 to 44. The next day the constitutional package passed 383 to 0 with 43 abstentions; Gennady Zyuganov said his deputies had voted against the reset. source · 2 · 3
On the posterDefend internet freedoms
ConsistentIts deputies refused to back the July 2025 law fining people for searching 'knowingly extremist' material online, which a party representative said breaches the Constitution; it passed 306 to 67, with 22 abstentions, on United Russia's votes. source · 2
Also worth knowing
  • On 15 February 2022 the Duma backed its draft, not United Russia's, asking the President to recognise the Donetsk and Luhansk 'republics' (351 votes); its deputy Kazbek Taysaev said it was 'time to stop the bloodshed in Donbas'. source
  • In March 2022 none of its deputies spoke against the law punishing 'fakes' about the army with up to 15 years, passed unanimously the same day in second and third reading. source
  • Its deputies were part of the unanimous Duma vote ratifying the annexation treaties of four Ukrainian regions in October 2022. source
To be fair
  • Opposed raising the pension age in 2018 with the rest of the parliamentary opposition (the reform passed 332 to 83)
  • Voted against the 2025 law fining people for searching 'extremist' material online (306 for, 67 against)
  • Fielded many of the 2026 election observers: most of the roughly 1,500 removed on the first day came from the CPRF and Yabloko (Atlas)

3 to 4 concrete promises from its latest programme, each checked against an official act or an independent tracker. “Made a difference” needs an independent measure of the effect on people; a promise adopted without measurable effect, symbolic or quickly reversed counts as “on paper” (a quarter of a point). “Partly” also covers a promise the party pushed but others blocked. A promise that needed power the party never held cannot be tested.

If you're considering voting for CPRF, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.
