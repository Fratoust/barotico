Egypt

Presidential republic under the 2014 Constitution (amended 2019) · about 109.5 million people

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Abdel Fattah al-Sisi's third term runs to 2030. The 2019 amendments lengthened terms to six years and reset his count, and the Constitution allows two terms, so he cannot run again unless it is amended. Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Citizens aged 18 and over are on the voter register through their national ID number; before each vote, look up your polling station with that number on the National Election Authority's service. Egyptians abroad vote at embassies and consulates, usually a few days before the vote in Egypt.

Last turnout: 32.41% in the 2025 House election (22,657,211 voters out of 69,891,913 registered); 17.1% in the August 2025 Senate election; 66.8% in the December 2023 presidential election source

Who decides what

PresidencyThe President is elected directly for six years (two terms at most since 2019), appoints the Prime Minister, can appoint up to 5% of the House and a third of the Senate, and may return laws to parliament. Abdel Fattah al-Sisi has held the office since 2014.
GovernmentPrime Minister Mostafa Madbouly leads a largely technocratic cabinet; the House approved a reshuffle with 13 new ministers in February 2026.
Two chambersThe House of Representatives (568 elected members plus up to 28 appointed) votes laws, the budget and the government; it elected the former head of the Central Auditing Organization, Hisham Badawi, as speaker in January 2026 by 521 votes to 49. The Senate (300 members, a third appointed) is mainly consultative.
Courts and electoral authorityThe Supreme Constitutional Court reviews laws; the Supreme Administrative Court and the Court of Cassation hear electoral appeals, and in 2025 they voided results in dozens of districts. The National Election Authority, run by judges, organises votes. Freedom House rates judicial independence 1 out of 4.
Governorates and local councilsGovernors are appointed by the President. Elected local councils have not been restored: the Egyptian Social Democratic Party noted in October 2025 that councils are still appointed and asked for a local administration law.

How people vote

  • House: half of the 568 elected seats (284) go to closed lists in 4 huge constituencies (100, 100, 42 and 42 seats), where the list with the most votes takes every seat; the other 284 are won in 143 individual districts. The President appoints up to 28 more. A list running alone only needs 5% of registered voters to turn out.
  • Senate: 100 list seats, 100 individual seats and 100 members appointed by the President; in August 2025 the single list won all 100 list seats on a 17.1% turnout.
  • The President is elected directly; in December 2023 Sisi won 89.6% on a 66.8% turnout. Egyptians abroad vote at embassies and consulates a few days before voters at home.

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from the President, the government or MPs; the cabinet drafts most major laws.
  2. The House debates and votes the text in committee and plenary; the Senate gives its opinion on bills referred to it.
  3. The President promulgates the law or sends it back: in September 2025 he returned the new Criminal Procedure Code, objecting to 8 articles, and the House rewrote them.
  4. The Supreme Constitutional Court can strike down a law; its ruling on frozen 'old rent' leases led to the 2025 rent law.

Why it's complicated

Egypt holds regular elections, but Freedom House scores them 0 out of 4 for the presidency and 1 out of 4 for parliament, and Human Rights Watch says the 2025 votes took place 'under severe repression and without genuine competition'. The closed-list system rewards one big coalition: in 2025 the Mostaqbal Watan-led National List, which included every party on this page, ran without a rival for the list seats. The individual seats were contested, so messily that the President's office asked for results that 'faithfully reflect the will of the voters'; the National Election Authority then voided 19 districts and the Supreme Administrative Court 29 more, and reruns stretched the election over 99 days (32.41% turnout). The Muslim Brotherhood, which won the 2011-2012 elections, is banned, and Amnesty counts about 6,000 people sent to 'terrorism' trials between September 2024 and May 2025. Courts are not independent in political cases, so this page notes when a rights body considers a conviction political. Parties are ranked by elected seats; the 9-card cap leaves out Al-Nour, the Conference Party, the Egyptian Freedom Party, Irada Geel and the Conservatives (1 to 4 seats each) and 105 independents.

Why it's like thisSince the 1952 revolution led by the Free Officers, Egypt has been run by a strong presidency backed by a dominant party (the Arab Socialist Union, then the National Democratic Party under Hosni Mubarak). The 2011 uprising ended Mubarak's 30-year rule; Mohamed Morsi of the Muslim Brotherhood was elected in 2012. In July 2013, after mass protests, the army led by defence minister Abdel Fattah al-Sisi removed him. The 2014 Constitution kept a strong presidency, and the 2019 amendments lengthened terms and recreated a Senate. Supporters present the system as a guarantee of stability after years of unrest and terrorism; critics see a return to the single-party era, a phrase the founders of the National Front party themselves used in 2025.

Who's at the wheel

President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi (no party), in office since 2014, re-elected in December 2023 for a term ending in 2030; Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly, whose cabinet was reshuffled with 13 new ministers in February 2026.

Sisi won 89.6% (39.7 million votes) on a 66.8% turnout, against Hazem Omar (4.5%), Farid Zahran (4.0%) and Abdel Sanad Yamama (1.9%), all three now on this page as party leaders. The government is appointed by the President and approved by the House, where Mostaqbal Watan and its allies hold a large majority.

The audience rating

Arab Barometer (wave IX, 1,225 face-to-face interviews, 11-25 September 2025): 72% call the economy bad or very bad, 85% say the government does badly at keeping prices down and 78% at creating jobs. The factsheet publishes no trust-in-government figure, and no independent national pollster publishes approval ratings of the President.
