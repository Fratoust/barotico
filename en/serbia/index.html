Serbia

Parliamentary republic · about 6.55 million people (RZS estimate, 2025)

Next vote≈ 1 monthsPresidential election (first round by 27 December 2026, not yet called on 13 November)
Aleksandar Vučić resigned as President on 27 September 2026 to campaign for parliament 'without holding office'. Ana Brnabić, Speaker of the outgoing Assembly, is acting President for at most three months. The vote must take place within three months of the vacancy, so 27 December at the latest, and it must be called 30 to 60 days in advance. On 30 October Brnabić gave 27 November as the deadline to call it, and said someone else could take over as acting President if the new institutions were formed quickly. The Assembly elected on 25 October 2026 holds its first session on 17 November and sits for four years. source

1 medical student stood on Aleksandar Vučić's list in 2026. The Student List, by its own candidate rules, had none.

The candidate criteria the students published in 2025 begin with 'Must not be a student!' (Vreme). Students explained that they lack experience and would instead oversee the MPs (Novosti, July 2026). The 'Aleksandar Vučić – Ujedinjena Srbija' list included medical student Miloš Pavlović (N1).

Who decides what

StateA single chamber, the Narodna skupština (250 MPs), passes laws and elects the government led by the Prime Minister. The President of the Republic is directly elected for five years (two terms at most), proposes the Prime Minister and can return a law once.
Courts and control bodiesThe Constitutional Court (Ustavni sud) reviews laws and decrees; the Supreme Court tops the courts. The State Audit Institution audits public money, the Agency for the Prevention of Corruption receives party accounts and asset declarations, and the Fiscal Council assesses the budget.
ProvincesVojvodina, in the north, is an autonomous province with its own assembly and government. The Constitution also names Kosovo as a province; Kosovo declared independence in 2008, which Serbia does not recognise.
Cities and municipalitiesBelgrade and the other cities and municipalities elect their assemblies, which then choose the mayor.
European UnionSerbia is an EU candidate country and negotiates accession; it is not a member and uses the dinar.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is tabled by the government, an MP, the Assembly of Vojvodina or 30,000 voters.
  2. The relevant committee examines it, then the Assembly debates it in general and article by article.
  3. Vote: a majority of MPs present, with at least half of all MPs in the chamber; some laws need an absolute majority of all 250.
  4. The President promulgates it or returns it once; the Constitutional Court can later annul it.

Political and economic context

On 1 November 2024, 16 people died when the canopy of the renovated Novi Sad railway station collapsed. Student-led protests followed for two years. Prime Minister Miloš Vučević resigned in January 2025 after an attack on students in Novi Sad, and Đuro Macut's government followed in April. Vučić announced snap elections more than 20 times before dissolving the Assembly on 9 September 2026; he then resigned the presidency to lead his party's list. On 25 October the Student List won an absolute majority and Vučić conceded. Observers noted strong competition but misuse of public resources and broadcasters favouring the ruling list. RIK's final results of 5 November, after repeat voting at 53 polling stations, confirmed the Student List's majority with 140 seats. As of 13 November, Macut's government stays in office until a new one is elected, the new Assembly meets on 17 November, and the presidential election, due by 27 December, has not yet been called; no prime minister candidate had been named by 10 November.

Why it's like thisMultiparty elections returned in 1990, under Slobodan Milošević. After the October 2000 protests that ended his rule, a parliamentary system with a directly elected president was kept. The current Constitution dates from 2006, the year Montenegro left the union with Serbia. Proportional lists in one national constituency were meant to give every vote the same weight, and minority lists were exempted from the threshold so that Hungarians, Bosniaks, Albanians and others are represented.
