Vietnam

One-party socialist republic led by the Communist Party of Vietnam · about 102.3 million people (2025)

Before you read
  • The approach: we set what parties say against what they do, and every fact links to an official or independent source.
  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.
  • Dates: the date at the top is the profile's last edit. Each section gives its own source and, where it applies, the date of the data or of the last check.
no free national vote≈ 50 monthsNo free national vote: the next formal national steps are the Communist Party's 15th Congress and a new National Assembly election, both due in 2031 at the end of the 2026-2031 term
No national office is filled by a free, competitive vote: Freedom House scores Vietnam 0 out of 4 for the election of the head of government, of the legislature and for the electoral framework. Power is allocated inside the Communist Party: its 14th Congress closed on 23 January 2026 and the new Central Committee re-elected Tô Lâm General Secretary the same day. Voters then elected a 500-seat National Assembly on 15 March 2026, which in April chose the President, the Prime Minister and the heads of the courts for 2026-2031. The next Congress and the next Assembly election are due when that term ends; no date has been set, so the countdown uses January 2031, by analogy with January 2026, as an estimate. Before that, the Assembly's current session closes on 20 November 2026. source

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. 63 provinces on 30 June 2025, 34 on 1 July.

On 12 June 2025, 461 of the 465 National Assembly deputies present voted to merge the country's provinces, and on 1 July 2025 63 provinces and cities became 34. The district level was abolished the same day and about 10,035 communes were merged into roughly 3,320; the government said at least 230,000 officials and employees would retire early or leave the public sector (Nhân Dân; Government portal).

Sources: Government of Viet Nam (VGP): two-tier local administrations from 1 July 2025, 63 provinces become 34 · Nhân Dân: National Assembly adopts the provincial merger, 461 votes of 465 (12 June 2025)

Who decides what

Communist PartyThe Communist Party of Vietnam (about 5.6 million members) leads the state. Its National Congress, the 14th of which closed on 23 January 2026, elects a Central Committee of 200 (180 full and 20 alternate members), which elects the 19-member Politburo and the General Secretary, Tô Lâm.
State: President and GovernmentThe President is elected by the National Assembly: on 7 April 2026 it elected General Secretary Tô Lâm by 495 votes to 0, so he now holds both posts. The Government is led by Prime Minister Lê Minh Hưng, elected the same day by 495 votes of 495 voting.
National AssemblyThe legislature has 500 deputies elected for five years; 18 of those elected in March 2026 (3.6%) are not Party members. It meets in sessions of a few weeks and elected Trần Thanh Mẫn as its Chair on 6 April 2026 by 491 votes of 491 voting.
Vietnam Fatherland Front (not a party)The Fatherland Front is an umbrella of mass organisations, not a party, and has no card on this page. In elections it runs the consultation rounds that decide who appears on the ballot: the UN Human Rights Committee notes that independent candidates must go through 'multiple rounds of negotiations' with it, and described it in 2019 as led by the Communist Party.
Provinces, communes and courtsSince 1 July 2025 local government has two tiers: 34 provinces and centrally run cities, and about 3,320 communes and wards, after the district level was abolished. The UN Human Rights Committee is 'concerned about the ruling party's influence on the judiciary'; Freedom House rates judicial independence 1 out of 4.

Sources: Đại biểu Nhân dân: results of the 14th Party Congress (Central Committee of 200, about 5.6 million members) · Supreme People's Court journal: the 19 Politburo members of the 14th term · National Assembly press releases No. 1 and No. 3, first session of the 16th legislature (6-7 April 2026) · UN Human Rights Committee, concluding observations on Viet Nam (July 2025), paras. 38 and 52

How a law is passed

  1. The National Assembly debates and votes laws in sessions of a few weeks: the session of 17 October-20 November 2026 is due to pass about 43 laws and resolutions, including a revised Criminal Code, Land Law and Labour Code.
  2. Major texts pass by very large majorities: the provincial merger of June 2025 was adopted by 461 of 465 deputies present.
  3. The law is then promulgated. The UN Human Rights Committee recommends measures 'to ensure the full independence and impartiality of the judiciary', which it finds under the ruling party's influence.

Sources: Hà Tĩnh provincial portal: second session of the 16th National Assembly, 17 October-20 November 2026 · Nhân Dân: vote on the provincial merger (12 June 2025) · UN Human Rights Committee (July 2025): ruling party's influence on the judiciary

Political and economic context

Vietnam has no free national election, and this page cannot rank parties by votes: the UN Human Rights Committee notes 'the ban on political parties other than the Communist Party of Viet Nam'. The only card is therefore the Communist Party's; the Vietnam Fatherland Front, which vets candidates, is an umbrella of mass organisations, not a party. Freedom House scores Vietnam 20 out of 100 (Not Free). The anti-corruption campaign is the dominant political story: Tô Lâm said in December 2025 that 174 centrally managed officials had been disciplined and 66 prosecuted in the 2021-2026 term, and 7 Politburo members, including two Presidents and a National Assembly Chair, left their posts. In July 2025 the UN Human Rights Committee welcomed the rise in prosecutions of senior officials but found that accountability mechanisms are 'often selectively applied and politically motivated', so this page flags that finding next to convictions. The same body counted at least 160 people reportedly jailed for peaceful expression; RSF counts 38 journalists behind bars, among them Phạm Đoan Trang, serving nine years for 'propaganda against the state'.

Sources: Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026: Viet Nam · UN Human Rights Committee, concluding observations (CCPR/C/VNM/CO/4, July 2025) · Báo Thanh tra: 174 centrally managed officials disciplined, 66 prosecuted (11 December 2025) · Báo Thanh tra: 7 Politburo members have left their posts in the 13th term (August 2024) · Đại biểu Nhân dân: 18 non-Party deputies among the 500 elected (March 2026) · RSF: one year of Tô Lâm in power (August 2025)

Why it's like thisThe Communist Party of Vietnam was founded in 1930 and its congress documents describe 1930-2030 as a century of national development under its leadership; the UN Human Rights Committee notes that no other party may be formed. Forty years ago the Party launched Đổi Mới ('renovation'), opening the economy to markets while keeping one-party rule, and its 2026 congress calls that line correct. Since 1990 Vietnam's Human Development Index has risen by 53.5% (UNDP). The same congress report also criticises a habit of 'talking a lot and doing little' and points to bottlenecks in institutions and discipline; critics, including UN treaty bodies, point to the ban on other parties and on independent media.

Sources: Nhân Dân: report on the documents of the 14th Congress (1930-2030, 40 years of Đổi Mới, 'talking a lot and doing little') · UNDP: Viet Nam's HDI up 53.5% since 1990 (May 2025) · UN Human Rights Committee (July 2025): ban on parties other than the Communist Party

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Vietnam has no competitive national election, so this page has a single party card, the Communist Party's; the Vietnam Fatherland Front is not a party and is described in the system blocks. The left-right axis does not apply: no expert survey places a ruling single party, so the card has no position. Vote shares and a rejection poll are left empty because candidates run without party labels and no independent political polling is published; the seat chart counts Party members as the 500 deputies minus the 18 officially announced non-Party deputies.

Several figures are missing or dated. The homicide gauge uses the latest available UNODC figure (2011). Population is the Population Department's 102.3 million. No approval or trust poll exists, the Party publishes no audited accounts, no independent promise tracker exists, and no official count of invalid ballots was published for 2026. The cabinet block does not link ministers to the Party card because the official list does not state party membership.

Press: with no independent domestic media, state outlets (the national news agency, Party, Assembly, government, court and inspectorate organs) are cited only for what the authorities announce, such as votes, verdicts and results. Findings on rights and the regime come from UN treaty bodies, Freedom House, RSF, UNDP and Transparency International. The judicial record lists only cases with an official act from the last ten years, within the site's caps, and notes the UN finding that accountability for officials is often applied selectively and for political ends; resignations without a criminal or disciplinary act are left out.
