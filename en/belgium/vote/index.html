Voting in Belgium

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Voting is compulsory from 18 for the Chamber and regional parliaments (16 for the European election). You can give a proxy for a valid reason (illness, work, studies, travel) with a certificate; otherwise justify your absence to the justice of the peace. Belgians abroad register with their consulate.

Last turnout: About 87.5% in June 2024: 12.5% of voters stayed away, a record despite compulsory voting source

The electoral system

  • Proportional representation by constituency, separate lists by language.
  • Compulsory voting for federal and regional elections.
  • Federal, regional and European elections on the same day, every 5 years.
Blank vote: what it says, what it changes

In Belgium, voting is compulsory (except in Flemish local elections since 2024). A blank vote is often the only legal way to choose no one.

What it is

  • Blank vote: a paper ballot with no mark at all. With electronic voting (as in Brussels), you tick the “blank vote” box. source
  • Spoilt vote: an invalid answer, such as a drawing on the paper. Electronic voting does not allow spoilt votes. source
  • Compulsory voting: adults must vote in federal, regional and (in Wallonia and Brussels) local elections. Flanders abolished the obligation for its local elections by a 2021 decree, applied in October 2024. source

How it is counted

  • Blank and spoilt votes are counted but not taken into account: seats are shared out among valid votes only. source
  • They do not “go to the majority”: they go to no party. But by reducing the number of valid votes, they raise every party's share. A party with 40% of all ballots reaches 50% of valid votes if 20% are blank or spoilt. source

The figures

  • 5.4%of voters cast a blank or spoilt ballot in 2019; almost 12% did not vote at all. source
  • 12.5%of voters stayed away in 2024 (over 1,050,000), a record; blank votes fell slightly, from 438,098 to 415,912. source

What it changes for the result

  • Where voting is compulsory, a blank ballot respects the law; staying home does not. source
  • Seats are allocated on valid votes only: blank and spoilt ballots change no party's seat count directly, but they make each valid vote weigh a little more. source

Questions to ask yourself

  • If no option represents me, do I want that refusal to be counted? Check above how it is counted here.
  • Do I reject every option, or is one of them less far from my ideas than the others?
  • Do I want to weigh on the result, or to make a refusal count?
  • Is the race close, is the country going through a crisis, could the election tip one way or the other? The closer it is, the more the outcome rests on the votes given to candidates.
  • Blank, spoilt or abstention: what does the law of this country do with each one?

Elsewhere, a blank vote has effects

  • FranceCounted separately and published since 2014, but outside the votes cast: no effect on the result or on thresholds. source
  • SpainBlank votes count as valid votes. They raise the number of votes a list needs to pass the 3% bar for seats. Only an empty envelope is blank: a blank sheet inside is spoilt. source source
  • ColombiaIf blank votes win a majority of valid votes (first presidential round, governors, mayors, assemblies), the election is held again once, without the same candidates. source
  • PeruIf spoilt and blank votes, together or separately, exceed two thirds of votes cast, the election is annulled. source
  • ItalyBlank ballots do not count as valid votes, but in abrogative referendums they count towards the turnout quorum: voting blank helps reach it, abstaining helps defeat it. source
  • Nevada (United States)Since 1975, ballots offer “None of these candidates” in statewide and presidential races. It can come first, but it never wins: the candidate with the most votes is elected. source
  • IndiaSince a 2013 Supreme Court ruling, voting machines offer “None of the above”. Some states (Maharashtra, Haryana) rerun local elections when that option comes first. source

For lawmakers and citizens who want to change the rule: each model has a cost. Rerunning an election takes time and money, a low threshold can leave seats empty, and counting blank votes changes majorities and thresholds.
