Country profile · 31 Oct 2026

Senegal

Presidential republic · about 18 million people · president and parliament at odds since May 2026

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

The president is elected for five years; Bassirou Diomaye Faye won on 24 March 2024, so the next vote is due in early 2029 (month estimated). Two earlier votes may come first: local councillors elected on 23 January 2022 see their terms expire on 23 January 2027, but the decree convening voters for the announced 17 January 2027 date was not signed by the 19 October deadline; and from early December 2026 the president may dissolve the National Assembly, which would trigger legislative elections within 60 to 90 days. source

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Register on the electoral rolls during a revision period announced by the Ministry of the Interior and the Direction générale des élections (DGE), with your national ECOWAS identity card, at your local revision commission or, abroad, at the consulate. On polling day bring the same card. Senegalese abroad vote in person in their country of residence and elect their own deputies.

Last turnout: 49.51% in the early legislative elections of 17 November 2024, after 61.30% in the presidential election of 24 March 2024 source

Who decides what

President of the RepublicElected by direct vote for five years. He appoints and dismisses the prime minister by decree (as Faye did on 22 May 2026), chairs the Council of Ministers, and from the third year of a legislature may dissolve the National Assembly after consulting the prime minister and the Assembly's president. If parliament does not pass the finance law in time, he can put it into force by decree (Article 68). Since April 2024: Bassirou Diomaye Faye.
Prime minister and governmentThe prime minister leads the government, presents a general policy statement to the Assembly and can be toppled by a censure motion (Article 86). Since 25 May 2026: Ahmadou Al Aminou Lô, a former BCEAO national director, with 30 ministers appointed on 1 June 2026.
National AssemblyA single chamber of 165 deputies elected for five years, installed on 2 December 2024. It votes laws and the budget, can censure the government and, by resolution, send ministers before the High Court of Justice (five former ministers in May 2025). The High Council of Local Authorities (HCCT) and the Economic, Social and Environmental Council were abolished in December 2024. Speaker since 26 May 2026: Ousmane Sonko.
Courts and auditThe Constitutional Council validates elections and reviews laws: it overturned the February 2024 postponement of the presidential election, part of the April 2025 amnesty 'interpretation' law and, in August and October 2026, two laws of the Pastef majority on special funds. The Supreme Court is the top ordinary court; the High Court of Justice tries ministers, without appeal. The Cour des comptes audits public accounts (its February 2025 report revealed the hidden debt).
Departments and communesDepartmental and municipal councils were elected on 23 January 2022 for five years; their renewal is now overdue in practice, with the presidential camp arguing the vote can take place at any point in 2027.

How people vote

  • Voting is voluntary from 18, for citizens registered on the electoral rolls kept by the Direction générale des élections; 7.37 million were registered in 2024. Voters abroad elect their own deputies.
  • The 165 deputies are elected in a mixed system: 112 by majority vote in the 46 departments and diaspora constituencies, where the list that comes first takes every seat, and 53 by national proportional representation. In 2024 Pastef turned 54.97% of the vote into 79% of the seats.
  • The president is elected in up to two rounds; Faye won in the first round in 2024 with 54.28%. The prime minister is not elected: the president appoints him.

How a law is passed

  1. Government bills are adopted in the Council of Ministers; deputies can table their own proposals (Pastef did so on special funds and the electoral code in 2026).
  2. A committee examines the text, then the plenary votes; a committee can reject a text, as the finance committee did with the 2026 amended budget on 23 October 2026.
  3. Before promulgation the president, the prime minister or deputies can refer a law to the Constitutional Council, which can strike it down in whole or in part.
  4. The president promulgates the law, which is published in the Journal officiel.

Why it's complicated

Senegal has two heads that no longer agree. Faye and Sonko won together in 2024; on 22 May 2026 the president dismissed his prime minister, Pastef refused to join the new government (five of its members joined anyway, unrecognised by the party), and Sonko now chairs an Assembly where Pastef holds 130 of 165 seats. In July 2026 Faye launched his own party, Kiiraay, which had no deputy as of September. The majority has blocked the 2026 amended budget in committee and threatens censure; from December 2026 the president may dissolve the Assembly. Meanwhile the Cour des comptes found that debt and the deficit had been under-reported under the previous government; debt reached about 132% of GDP at the end of 2024 and an IMF programme of 2.2 billion dollars awaits Board approval. Freedom House rates Senegal Free (70/100) but scores judicial independence 2 of 4 and notes politically motivated prosecutions of opposition leaders in recent years.

Why it's like thisSenegal became independent in 1960 and has never had a military coup. A one-party period gave way to multiparty politics, and power changed hands at the ballot box in 2000, 2012 and 2024. The 2021-2024 years were tense: protests around the prosecutions of opposition leader Ousmane Sonko, the dissolution of his party Pastef in 2023, and a decree postponing the February 2024 presidential election, which the Constitutional Council annulled. An amnesty law adopted on 6 March 2024 covered offences linked to demonstrations or political motives between February 2021 and February 2024; it freed Sonko and Faye, and Faye won the presidency three weeks later. The strong presidency and the dissolution power date from earlier constitutions; the current clash between president and Assembly is the first test of them by a single party split in two.

Who's at the wheel

President Bassirou Diomaye Faye (elected March 2024 as Pastef's candidate; in July 2026 he launched his own party, Kiiraay – Les Patriotes Républicains). Prime Minister Ahmadou Al Aminou Lô since 25 May 2026, at the head of 30 ministers named on 1 June: Cheikh Diba (Economy, Finance and Planning), Moussa Sarr (Justice), Mohamedou Makhtar Cissé (Interior), Cheikh Niang (Foreign Affairs), Yankhoba Diémé (Armed Forces). Pastef, the majority party, is not part of it.

Faye dismissed Sonko by decree on 22 May 2026 and appointed Lô three days later. Pastef refused to take part, saying it wanted guarantees on justice, contract renegotiation and debt and at least half the portfolios. Lô gave his general policy statement on 8 September 2026; no censure motion was tabled, but Pastef rejected his amended 2026 budget in committee on 23 October and the plenary sent it back to committee on 27 October.

The results, as measured by independent bodies

Findings from independent public research bodies. No label, no verdict: the figures speak.

  • Cour des comptes (Feb. 2025): debt of 99.67% of GDP at end-2023, not the 74.41% announced; 2023 deficit of 12.3% of GDP, not 4.9%; 2,517 billion FCFA of bank debt contracted outside the budget. source
  • IMF figures reported in September 2026: public debt about 132% of GDP at end-2024 and 125% at end-2025; about a quarter of tax revenue goes to interest; energy subsidies could exceed 800 billion FCFA in 2026 against about 300 billion budgeted. source
  • ANSD: GDP grew 6.7% in 2025 (6.5% in 2024), but only 2.2% excluding hydrocarbons. source
  • ANSD: broad unemployment (including discouraged job-seekers) at 23.3% in Q4 2025, up from 20.0% a year earlier; 27.4% among 15-35 year-olds. source

  • Public: RTS (TV and radio), the daily Le Soleil and the national agency APS
  • Groupe Futurs Médias: TFM television and RFM radio, both briefly taken off air in October 2025
  • Groupe Walfadjri: Walf TV, whose licence was withdrawn by the minister in February 2024 and restored a week later

source

Documented facts to know

  • RSF 2026: Senegal 78th (58.11), down from 74th in 2025. source
  • Under the Sall government, 4 February 2024: the Communication minister cut Walf TV's signal and withdrew its licence, citing 'subversive' remarks; RSF said the minister lacked the power and the CNRA never met; restored on 11 February. source
  • Under the Faye-Sonko government, 28-30 October 2025: journalists Maïmouna Ndour Faye (7TV) and Babacar Fall (RFM) were arrested after interviewing Madiambal Diagne, and 7TV and TFM were taken off air without a CNRA decision; both journalists were released. source
  • Ministry of Communication registry: 112 of 380 outlets declared compliant with the press code in December 2024, 258 of 639 in February 2025; an April 2025 order told the others to stop publishing. source
  • Supreme Court, 11 December 2025: on a publishers' (CDEPS) appeal, it annulled the ministerial order and the commission vetting outlets for the press support fund, after suspending the cessation notice against Public SN in June. source
