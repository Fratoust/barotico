Argentina

Federal presidential republic · about 46 million people

4 chiefs of cabinet in under three years.

Nicolás Posse, Guillermo Francos, Manuel Adorni and, since 30 June 2026, Diego Santilli. In Argentina's presidential system the Chief of Cabinet answers to Congress but is appointed and removed by the President alone, so the job changes hands without any vote.

Who decides what

Nation: President and CongressThe President is head of state and government for four years, renewable once in a row, and can issue decrees of necessity and urgency (DNU) that stand unless both chambers reject them. Congress has two chambers: 257 deputies and 72 senators.
CourtsThe Supreme Court (Corte Suprema de Justicia de la Nación) has the last word; federal judges in Buenos Aires (the Comodoro Py courthouse) handle corruption cases involving national officials.
23 provinces and Buenos Aires CityEach province has its own constitution, elected governor and legislature, and runs schools, hospitals and police. Governors weigh heavily in Congress because their deputies and senators often vote as they say.
MunicipalitiesMayors (intendentes) and councils run local services; their powers depend on each province's constitution.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill can start in either chamber (tax bills start with the deputies), sent by the President or by members of Congress.
  2. It goes through committees and a floor vote, then to the other chamber, which can approve, amend or reject it.
  3. The President promulgates it or vetoes it in whole or in part. Congress can override a veto with two-thirds in both chambers.
  4. Short cut: a decree of necessity and urgency (DNU) takes effect at once and only falls if both chambers reject it.

Why it's complicated

Javier Milei (La Libertad Avanza) won the presidency in 2023 with a tiny party, then won the October 2025 midterm with 40.8% of the vote; since December 2025 his bloc is the largest in the Chamber (95 of 257) but still short of a majority in either chamber. With governors' votes he passed a labour reform in February 2026. Inflation, which fell from 211% in 2023, was back at 33.4% a year in September 2026, poverty rose to 32.3% and unemployment to 7.9%. Corruption cases reach both camps: former President Cristina Fernández de Kirchner, who formally still chairs the Peronist party but cannot exercise the post, is serving a final six-year sentence under house arrest and is on trial again; on the government side, the $LIBRA token and the disability-agency (ANDIS) cases are under investigation, and Chief of Cabinet Manuel Adorni resigned in June 2026 while under investigation for illicit enrichment.

Why it's like thisThe 1853 Constitution, inspired by the United States, created a federal presidential republic to hold together provinces that had fought each other for decades. The 20th century alternated elected governments with military coups; democracy returned in 1983 after the last dictatorship. The 1994 reform, agreed between Peronists and Radicals, cut the presidential term to four years with one re-election, added the run-off, a third senator per province and the Chief of Cabinet, gave Buenos Aires City autonomy and recognised the President's emergency decrees.

Who's at the wheel

President Javier Milei (La Libertad Avanza) since 10 December 2023, with Vice-President Victoria Villarruel, Chief of Cabinet Diego Santilli (since 30 June 2026) and Economy Minister Luis Caputo.

Milei won the 2023 run-off. In the 26 October 2025 midterm La Libertad Avanza came first nationally with 40.8%, ahead of the Peronist Fuerza Patria. Its bloc grew to 95 deputies and, by February 2026, 21 senators, with the PRO as its main ally; it still needs governors and dialogue blocs to pass laws.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Registration is automatic with the national ID (DNI); check your polling place on the electoral register (padrón) published by the Cámara Nacional Electoral before each vote. Bring your DNI. Those who do not vote must justify it or pay a fine. Argentines abroad can register at their consulate to vote in national elections.

Last turnout: 67.85% in the October 2025 legislative election, the lowest since democracy returned in 1983 (81% in the 2015 presidential election) source

The electoral system

  • Voting is compulsory from 18 to 70 and optional at 16-17 and over 70. Since 2025 national elections use a single paper ballot (Boleta Única de Papel) listing every party.
  • The President needs 45% of the vote, or 40% with a 10-point lead, to win in the first round; otherwise the top two meet in a run-off.
  • Half of the deputies are renewed every two years, by proportional lists in each province. Each province elects three senators (two for the winner, one for the runner-up), and a third of the Senate is renewed every two years.
