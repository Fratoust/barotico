Federal presidential republic · about 46 million people

The law sets the general election on the fourth Sunday of October. In 2027 voters choose the President and Vice-President, 130 deputies, 24 senators, 21 governors and the head of government of Buenos Aires City. A run-off follows if nobody wins in the first round. The government wants to suspend the open primaries (PASO) for 2027, but in October 2026 it still lacked the 37 Senate votes needed. source

Voting in Argentina

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Registration is automatic with the national ID (DNI); check your polling place on the electoral register (padrón) published by the Cámara Nacional Electoral before each vote. Bring your DNI. Those who do not vote must justify it or pay a fine. Argentines abroad can register at their consulate to vote in national elections.

Last turnout: 67.85% in the October 2025 legislative election, the lowest since democracy returned in 1983 (81% in the 2015 presidential election) source

The electoral system

  • Voting is compulsory from 18 to 70 and optional at 16-17 and over 70. Since 2025 national elections use a single paper ballot (Boleta Única de Papel) listing every party.
  • The President needs 45% of the vote, or 40% with a 10-point lead, to win in the first round; otherwise the top two meet in a run-off.
  • Half of the deputies are renewed every two years, by proportional lists in each province. Each province elects three senators (two for the winner, one for the runner-up), and a third of the Senate is renewed every two years.
Blank vote: what it says, what it changes

In Argentina, voting is compulsory from 18 to 70. Since 2025, with the single paper ballot, a blank vote means leaving a category unmarked.

What it is

  • Blank vote: no preference marked in one or more categories of the ballot. source
  • Null vote: marks for different lists in the same category, a ballot torn so the choice cannot be read, writing that hides the choice, or foreign objects inside. source
  • Not voting without a valid reason brings a fine of 50 to 500 pesos and, if unpaid, restrictions such as being barred from public posts for three years. source

How it is counted

  • Blank votes are counted separately from null votes. Because the ballot lists several categories, a voter can choose in one and leave another blank. source

What it changes for the result

  • Where voting is compulsory, a blank vote is the legal way to go to the polls without choosing; staying home exposes you to a fine. source

Questions to ask yourself

  • If no option represents me, do I want that refusal to be counted? Check above how it is counted here.
  • Do I reject every option, or is one of them less far from my ideas than the others?
  • Do I want to weigh on the result, or to make a refusal count?
  • Is the race close, is the country going through a crisis, could the election tip one way or the other? The closer it is, the more the outcome rests on the votes given to candidates.
  • Blank, spoilt or abstention: what does the law of this country do with each one?

Barotico does not recommend voting blank, nor avoiding it. These questions are there to help you decide with the facts in hand.

Elsewhere, a blank vote has effects

  • FranceCounted separately and published since 2014, but outside the votes cast: no effect on the result or on thresholds. source
  • SpainBlank votes count as valid votes. They raise the number of votes a list needs to pass the 3% bar for seats. Only an empty envelope is blank: a blank sheet inside is spoilt. source source
  • ColombiaIf blank votes win a majority of valid votes (first presidential round, governors, mayors, assemblies), the election is held again once, without the same candidates. source
  • PeruIf spoilt and blank votes, together or separately, exceed two thirds of votes cast, the election is annulled. source
  • ItalyBlank ballots do not count as valid votes, but in abrogative referendums they count towards the turnout quorum: voting blank helps reach it, abstaining helps defeat it. source
  • Nevada (United States)Since 1975, ballots offer “None of these candidates” in statewide and presidential races. It can come first, but it never wins: the candidate with the most votes is elected. source
  • IndiaSince a 2013 Supreme Court ruling, voting machines offer “None of the above”. Some states (Maharashtra, Haryana) rerun local elections when that option comes first. source

For lawmakers and citizens who want to change the rule: each model has a cost. Rerunning an election takes time and money, a low threshold can leave seats empty, and counting blank votes changes majorities and thresholds.
