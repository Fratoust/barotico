Constitutional monarchy, parliamentary system · about 69 million people

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Register online on GOV.UK ('Register to vote'), at the latest 12 working days before polling day. Bring photo ID to the polling station for UK Parliament elections. Postal and proxy votes are available on request. British citizens living abroad can register as overseas electors, with no time limit since 2024.

Last turnout: 59.7% in the July 2024 general election (28.8 million valid votes, 48.2 million registered) source

Who decides what

UK Parliament and Government (Westminster)650 elected MPs and an appointed House of Lords. The Prime Minister governs as long as he keeps a Commons majority. Covers defence, foreign affairs, most taxes and everything for England, which has no parliament of its own. The King is head of state with a ceremonial role.
Scottish Parliament and Government129 MSPs in Edinburgh: health, schools, justice, some taxes. After the May 2026 election the SNP (58 seats) governs without a majority, led by John Swinney.
Senedd and Welsh Government96 Members since 2026 (60 before): health, education, transport. In May 2026 Plaid Cymru became the largest party (43 seats); its leader Rhun ap Iorwerth was elected First Minister on 12 May. Reform UK (34) is the official opposition.
Northern Ireland Assembly and Executive90 MLAs. Power-sharing is compulsory: the largest unionist and nationalist parties must govern together, under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement.
Councils and mayorsLocal councils run social care, bins, planning and (in part) schools. England also has elected regional mayors, such as the Mayor of Greater Manchester, the job Andy Burnham held before becoming Prime Minister.

How people vote

  • Westminster uses first-past-the-post: each of the 650 constituencies elects one MP, and whoever gets the most votes wins, even without a majority. In 2024 Labour won 411 of 650 seats (63%) with about a third of the vote.
  • The devolved parliaments are more proportional: Scotland mixes constituencies and regional lists; Wales, since 2026, uses closed party lists counted with the D'Hondt formula; Northern Ireland uses the single transferable vote.
  • Voting age is 18 for Westminster and 16 for Scottish and Welsh elections. Photo ID is required at polling stations for UK Parliament elections.
  • Nobody elects the House of Lords: its members are mostly appointed for life, plus 26 Church of England bishops. Labour pledged in 2024 to remove the remaining hereditary peers, and the May 2026 King's Speech announced a law to allow peerages to be taken away.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is introduced in the Commons or the Lords; most government bills start in the Commons.
  2. Each House debates it in stages: second reading, committee (line by line), report, third reading.
  3. Amendments shuttle between the two Houses ('ping-pong') until they agree.
  4. The Lords can delay most Commons bills by about a year (one month for money bills) but cannot block them, under the Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949.
  5. Royal Assent makes it an Act. By convention, Westminster does not legislate on devolved matters without the consent of the devolved parliament.

Why it's complicated

The 2024 election gave Labour a huge majority on a third of the vote, and voters have kept moving since. Reform UK led the polls for much of 2025; Labour lost by-elections to Reform (2025) and the Greens (2026). In May 2026 the SNP held Scotland without a majority, Plaid Cymru overtook Labour in Wales, and Labour suffered heavy losses in the English local elections. Keir Starmer resigned in June 2026; Andy Burnham, elected Labour leader unopposed, became Prime Minister on 20 July. By October, Ipsos had Labour on 32%, the Conservatives 24%, Reform 20%, the Lib Dems 9% and the Greens 8%.

Why it's like thisThe UK has no single written constitution: its rules come from Acts of Parliament, court rulings and conventions, and Parliament is sovereign. First-past-the-post grew out of centuries of single-member seats and usually produces one-party majorities. Scotland and Wales got their own parliaments after the 1997 referendums; Northern Ireland's power-sharing comes from the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, which ended decades of conflict. The House of Lords is the descendant of a medieval council of nobles and bishops, whose powers were cut by the Parliament Acts of 1911 and 1949.
