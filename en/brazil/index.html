Brazil

Federal presidential republic · 213.4 million people (IBGE, 2025)

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

You need a voter card (título de eleitor), obtained from the electoral courts or online, and registration closes about five months before election day. Voting is compulsory from 18 to 70: if you miss it, you must justify your absence (for example in the e-Título app) or pay a small fine. Brazilians abroad register with a consulate and can vote only for President.

Last turnout: 80.8% in the presidential runoff of 25 October 2026 (abstention 19.22%, the lowest in a presidential race since 2006); abstention was 21.08% in the first round source

Who decides what

Federal level (União)The President is both head of state and head of government and appoints the ministers. The National Congress has two chambers: the Chamber of Deputies (513) and the Federal Senate (81, three per state). No party comes close to a majority, so presidents govern with coalitions.
26 states and the Federal DistrictEach elects a governor and a legislative assembly. States run the police, secondary schools and much of health care. Governors such as Tarcísio de Freitas in São Paulo are national players.
MunicipalitiesMore than 5,500 municipalities, each with an elected mayor and council, in charge of primary schools, basic health care and urban services. Municipal elections take place two years after general elections (next in 2028).
Higher courtsThe Supreme Federal Court (STF, 11 justices) is both the constitutional court and the trial court for the president, ministers and members of Congress: that is why it tried Jair Bolsonaro directly. The Superior Electoral Court (TSE) runs elections and can ban candidates.

How people vote

  • The President and governors are elected in two rounds: if nobody gets more than half of the valid votes on the first Sunday of October, the top two meet in a runoff on the last Sunday of October.
  • Deputies are elected by open-list proportional representation in each state (from 8 to 70 seats per state): voters pick a candidate or a party, and a party's votes decide how many of its most-voted candidates get in. Electoral coalitions are banned for these seats since 2020, but parties may form 'federations' that must last the whole four-year term.
  • Senators serve eight years; one third or two thirds of the Senate is renewed alternately every four years by simple majority (two seats per state in 2026).
  • Voting is compulsory from 18 to 70 and optional at 16-17 and after 70. Everyone votes on electronic voting machines, and results are usually known on election night.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill can be tabled by the President, any member of Congress, the higher courts in their own field, or citizens (a popular initiative signed by 1% of voters).
  2. It goes through committees and the plenary of the Chamber and then of the Senate; if the second chamber changes it, it returns to the first.
  3. The President signs or vetoes it, in whole or in part. Congress can override a veto by an absolute majority of both chambers in a joint session, as it did with the 'Dosimetria' law in April 2026.
  4. The President can also issue provisional measures (medidas provisórias) that apply at once but lapse after 120 days unless Congress approves them.
  5. Constitutional amendments need three fifths of each chamber in two rounds: the 'Shield PEC' passed the Chamber in September 2025 but was buried unanimously by the Senate's justice committee.

Why it's complicated

Brazil re-elected a left-wing president and, on the same days, its most right-leaning Congress in decades. Lula won the runoff by 4.4 million votes after Flávio Bolsonaro led the first round, but the parties whose national leaderships backed Flávio in the runoff (PL, União Brasil, PP, Republicanos, MDB, Podemos, Novo) won 322 of 513 seats, and the PL alone 121, the largest group since the 1988 Constitution. The opposition's founding figure, Jair Bolsonaro, is serving a 27-year sentence for the 2022 coup plot, under humanitarian house arrest since March 2026; Congress then passed, over Lula's veto, a law that can reduce such sentences, now challenged at the Supreme Court. Meanwhile Congress controls a growing slice of the budget through parliamentary amendments (R$31.5bn paid in 2025), which makes every presidential coalition expensive.

Why it's like thisThe 1988 Constitution was written after 21 years of military dictatorship (1964-85), so it spreads power widely: a strong president, but a powerful Congress, autonomous states and municipalities, and a Supreme Court with broad powers, including trying top politicians itself. Proportional voting with open lists in each state produced dozens of parties; presidents have since governed through broad and costly coalitions, a model Brazilian political scientists call 'coalition presidentialism'. Voters kept the presidential system in a 1993 referendum. Reforms in 2017 banned coalitions for proportional seats and set a performance threshold to reduce the number of parties, which led to the creation of party federations.

Who's at the wheel

President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (PT) since 1 January 2023, with Vice-President Geraldo Alckmin (PSB). Re-elected on 25 October 2026, he starts a fourth term on 5 January 2027.

Lula beat Jair Bolsonaro in 2022 with 50.90% of the vote and governed with a broad coalition that gave ministries to centre and centre-right parties (MDB, PSD, União Brasil, PP, Republicanos). União Brasil and PP formally left the government in September 2025. In 2026 he was re-elected with a record 64.3 million votes (51.79%).
