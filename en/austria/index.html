Austria

Federal parliamentary republic · 9.2 million people · 9 Länder

155 days passed between the September 2024 election and the swearing-in of a government, led by the party that came second.

The FPÖ came first with 28.85%. Its leader Herbert Kickl received the mandate to form a government, but talks with the ÖVP collapsed over who would get which ministries, and he handed the mandate back on 12 February 2025. On 3 March 2025 ÖVP, SPÖ and NEOS took office together.

Who decides what

Federal (Bund)The Nationalrat (183 MPs) makes federal law and can bring down the government; the Bundesrat represents the Länder; the directly elected Federal President appoints the chancellor and can dismiss the government.
States (Länder)9 Länder, each with its own parliament (Landtag) and government led by a governor (Landeshauptmann). Vienna is both a Land and a city.
MunicipalitiesAbout 2,100 municipalities with elected councils and mayors run local services, zoning and kindergartens.
European UnionAustria has been an EU member since 1995 and elects 20 MEPs; its budget is under the EU excessive deficit procedure.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill comes from the government, MPs, the Bundesrat or a citizens' initiative (Volksbegehren): one signed by 100,000 voters must be debated by the Nationalrat.
  2. The Nationalrat examines it in committee and votes in plenary, usually in three readings.
  3. The Bundesrat can object, but in most cases the Nationalrat can override it with a second vote.
  4. The Federal President certifies the law. Constitutional laws need a two-thirds majority, and the Constitutional Court (VfGH) can strike laws down.

Why it's complicated

The FPÖ won the 2024 election with 28.85% but sits in opposition. ÖVP and SPÖ together had 92 of 183 seats, a majority of one, so they brought in NEOS: the coalition holds 110 seats. It inherited a 4.6% deficit and an EU excessive deficit procedure; the Fiscal Council said in June 2026 that the 3% target will be missed. In May 2026 the ÖVP's parliamentary leader August Wöginger was convicted at first instance of abuse of office and resigned (appeal pending). Only 25% said they trust the federal government (April 2026).

Why it's like thisThe 1920 constitution, drafted largely by the jurist Hans Kelsen, created one of the world's first constitutional courts and a federal state of Länder. After the civil war of 1934, dictatorship and annexation by Nazi Germany in 1938, the Second Republic (1945) was built on consensus: long 'grand coalitions' of ÖVP and SPÖ, a powerful 'social partnership' of employers and unions, and permanent neutrality from 1955. A 1929 reform strengthened the directly elected president, who appoints and can dismiss the government.

Who's at the wheel

Chancellor Christian Stocker (ÖVP) leads an ÖVP-SPÖ-NEOS coalition since 3 March 2025, with Andreas Babler (SPÖ) as vice-chancellor, Beate Meinl-Reisinger (NEOS) as foreign minister and Markus Marterbauer (SPÖ) as finance minister.

The Federal President appoints the chancellor and, on the chancellor's proposal, the ministers; no investiture vote is needed. The Nationalrat can remove the government by a no-confidence vote, as it did for the first time in May 2019 after the Ibiza video.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Austrian citizens aged 16+ on the electoral roll can vote at their polling station or request a voting card (Wahlkarte) to vote by post or anywhere in Austria. Austrians living abroad must register in the electoral roll of their last municipality in Austria (or the one linked to their family) and can then vote by post.

Last turnout: 77.68% in the September 2024 Nationalrat election (+2.09 points on 2019) source

The electoral system

  • One vote for a party list, plus optional preference votes for candidates. Seats are shared out in proportion across regional, state and federal levels.
  • A party needs 4% nationally (or a seat won in a regional constituency) to enter the Nationalrat. In 2024 five parties made it; KPÖ and the Bierpartei fell short.
  • Citizens vote from age 16, one of the lowest voting ages in the EU. The Federal President is elected directly; if no one gets an absolute majority, a runoff follows four weeks later.
