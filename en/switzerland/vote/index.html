Voting in Switzerland

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Swiss citizens aged 18+ are registered automatically in their commune and receive their ballot papers by post; most vote by mail, some cantons also allow e-voting trials. Swiss living abroad register with their embassy or consulate and can vote by post in federal elections and votes.

Last turnout: 46.6% in the October 2023 National Council election source

The electoral system

  • National Council: seats shared out by canton according to population, at least one each, and elected by proportional representation in most cantons. Council of States: mostly majority vote; Jura and Neuchâtel use proportional representation.
  • 100,000 signatures in 18 months force a national vote on a constitutional amendment (popular initiative); it needs a majority of voters and of cantons.
  • Turnout is often below half: 46.6% in the 2023 federal election, 47% for the September 2026 ballots, 58.9% for the June 2026 vote on population growth.

Sources: Federal Chancellery: The Confederation in brief 2026: elections, initiatives, double majority · Parliament fact sheet: the popular initiative · SRF (SRG SSR): turnout in the 2023 federal election · gfs.bern: VOX analysis of the June 2026 votes · SRF (SRG SSR): the 27 September 2026 votes

Blank vote: what it says, what it changes

In Switzerland, people vote several times a year on laws and initiatives. Blank ballots are counted, but they do not enter the yes-no result.

What it is

  • Blank ballot: a ballot handed in without a yes or a no. source
  • Void ballot: not official, not filled in by hand, intention not clearly recognisable, or carrying defamatory remarks or obviously irrelevant markings. source

How it is counted

  • In the official results, blank and void ballots are listed as “not taken into account”; only valid ballots, the yes and the no, decide. source

The figures

  • 1.7%of ballots were blank in the March 2012 vote on second homes (38,798), more than the margin by which the initiative passed (28,796 votes). source

What it changes for the result

  • In 2012, the second-homes initiative passed with 1,152,598 yes against 1,123,802 no. The blank ballots outnumbered the gap: on a close vote, those who decide are those who choose yes or no. source

Questions to ask yourself

  • If no option represents me, do I want that refusal to be counted? Check above how it is counted here.
  • Do I reject every option, or is one of them less far from my ideas than the others?
  • Do I want to weigh on the result, or to make a refusal count?
  • Is the race close, is the country going through a crisis, could the election tip one way or the other? The closer it is, the more the outcome rests on the votes given to candidates.
  • Blank, spoilt or abstention: what does the law of this country do with each one?

Barotico does not recommend voting blank, nor avoiding it. These questions are there to help you decide with the facts in hand.

Elsewhere, a blank vote has effects

  • FranceCounted separately and published since 2014, but outside the votes cast: no effect on the result or on thresholds. source
  • SpainBlank votes count as valid votes. They raise the number of votes a list needs to pass the 3% bar for seats. Only an empty envelope is blank: a blank sheet inside is spoilt. source source
  • ColombiaIf blank votes win a majority of valid votes (first presidential round, governors, mayors, assemblies), the election is held again once, without the same candidates. source
  • PeruIf spoilt and blank votes, together or separately, exceed two thirds of votes cast, the election is annulled. source
  • ItalyBlank ballots do not count as valid votes, but in abrogative referendums they count towards the turnout quorum: voting blank helps reach it, abstaining helps defeat it. source
  • Nevada (United States)Since 1975, ballots offer “None of these candidates” in statewide and presidential races. It can come first, but it never wins: the candidate with the most votes is elected. source
  • IndiaSince a 2013 Supreme Court ruling, voting machines offer “None of the above”. It cannot cancel an election: the candidate with the most votes still wins. source

For lawmakers and citizens who want to change the rule: each model has a cost. Rerunning an election takes time and money, a low threshold can leave seats empty, and counting blank votes changes majorities and thresholds.

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Six party cards cover the parties with their own parliamentary group. The EVP (2.0%), the EDU (1.2%), the Lega and the MCG won less than 3% and sit in other groups, so they have no card. National Council seats in 'Who holds power' are derived from the official 2023 result and the group changes listed by parliament, because parliament's pages give no current count per group; the Council of States, which has only informal groups, is not shown.

Left-right positions are an estimate of the 2019 Chapel Hill Expert Survey scale, as the survey files could not be opened; no party reaches the far-left threshold, and the SVP is labelled far right on that estimate. No national 'would never vote for' poll was found, so rejection figures are missing. Shares of registered voters are estimated from party strength times turnout, because Swiss voters can split their votes across lists and no national vote count per party exists.

There is no independent promise tracker: the promise-o-meter relies on parliamentary votes, official results and public statistics. Party finance shows declared totals only; the Federal Audit Office publishes the details, but they were not checked against a validated source. Press links come only from the public broadcaster SRG SSR (including swissinfo.ch), the national agency Keystone-SDA (also when republished with a visible credit) and official bodies; claims backed only by other outlets were rewritten or dropped. Cases without a formal act, such as refused immunity requests, are not listed.
