Switzerland

Federal republic with direct democracy · about 9 million people · 26 cantons

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Swiss citizens aged 18+ are registered automatically in their commune and receive their ballot papers by post; most vote by mail, some cantons also allow e-voting trials. Swiss living abroad register with their embassy or consulate and can vote by post in federal elections and votes.

Last turnout: 46.6% in the October 2023 National Council election source

Who decides what

The peopleCitizens have the last word: they vote on constitutional amendments, on popular initiatives and, if 50,000 sign for a referendum, on laws passed by parliament.
Federal (Confederation)The Federal Assembly has two equal chambers: the National Council (200 seats, by population) and the Council of States (46 seats, two per canton, one per half-canton). It elects the seven-member Federal Council, which governs collectively.
Cantons26 cantons, each with its own constitution, parliament, government, courts and taxes; they run schools, police and hospitals.
CommunesAbout 2,100 communes, many of which still decide budgets and local taxes in citizens' assemblies or local votes.

How people vote

  • National Council: proportional representation by canton, with lists that voters can edit (cross out, add, double names). Council of States: rules set by each canton, mostly majority vote.
  • 100,000 signatures in 18 months force a national vote on a constitutional amendment (popular initiative); it needs a majority of voters and of cantons.
  • Turnout is often below half: 46.6% in the 2023 federal election, 47% for the September 2026 ballots, 58.9% for the June 2026 vote on population growth.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill comes from the Federal Council, a member of parliament, a canton or a popular initiative. Drafts first go out for consultation to cantons, parties and associations.
  2. Both chambers must pass the same text; differences shuttle back and forth until they agree.
  3. If 50,000 citizens sign within 100 days, the law goes to a popular vote; constitutional changes always do.
  4. There is no constitutional court that can strike down federal laws: the last check is the people.

Why it's complicated

All four big parties sit in government at once, so there is no real opposition bench, yet each party campaigns against the government whenever it likes. The SVP came first in 2023 with 27.9%, but lost its two big 2026 ballots (population cap 45.2%, neutrality 29.8%). Guy Parmelin (SVP) leaves the Federal Council at the end of 2026. Parliament is still working through the EU treaty package, with a vote planned for June 2027. US tariffs of up to 39% hit exporters in 2025, and health premiums rise 5% in 2027.

Why it's like thisModern Switzerland was founded in 1848 as a federation of sovereign cantons with four languages and two main religions after a short civil war. To keep such diversity together, power was split between cantons and the centre, and citizens gained the right to referendums (1874) and popular initiatives (1891). Since 1959 the largest parties share the seven government seats by an unwritten 'magic formula', today 2 SVP, 2 SP, 2 FDP, 1 Mitte, so that no big group can block everything by referendum.

Who's at the wheel

The seven-member Federal Council: Guy Parmelin (SVP, economy, President in 2026), Albert Rösti (SVP, environment, transport, energy), Karin Keller-Sutter (FDP, finance), Ignazio Cassis (FDP, foreign affairs), Elisabeth Baume-Schneider (SP, home affairs and health), Beat Jans (SP, justice and police) and Martin Pfister (Mitte, defence). Parmelin steps down at the end of 2026.

Parliament elects each Federal Councillor for four years; there is no prime minister, no coalition agreement and no vote of no confidence. The presidency rotates every year among the seven. Decisions are taken collectively and defended by all, even by those who voted against. Parmelin's successor will be elected on 9 December 2026.
