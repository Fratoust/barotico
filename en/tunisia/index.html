Tunisia

Presidential republic under the 2022 Constitution · about 12 million people

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Holders of a national identity card are on the ISIE register; check your polling station on the ISIE's channels before each vote and update your address if you have moved. Voting is not compulsory. Tunisians abroad vote at consulates and elect their own deputies.

Last turnout: 28.8% in the October 2024 presidential election (2,808,548 voters out of 9,753,217 registered); 11.4% in the second round of the 2023 legislative elections source

Who decides what

PresidencyThe President is elected for five years (two terms at most), sets national policy, appoints and dismisses the head of government and ministers, and can legislate by decree when parliament is not sitting. Kaïs Saïed, elected in 2019, was re-elected in October 2024.
GovernmentThe head of government, Sara Zaafrani Zenzri since March 2025, carries out the President's policy and is responsible to him; the two chambers together can censure it only by a qualified majority.
Two chambersThe Assembly of the Representatives of the People (ARP, 161 deputies, elected individually in 2022-2023) and the National Council of Regions and Districts (indirectly elected from local councils) vote laws and the budget.
Courts and electoral authorityThe 2022 Constitution calls justice a 'function' rather than a power. Freedom House rates judicial independence 1 out of 4. The ISIE runs elections; in September 2024 parliament moved electoral disputes from the Administrative Court to the ordinary courts.
Regions, districts and municipalitiesLocal councils elected in 2023-2024 send members to regional and district councils. Municipal councils await new elections, which the ISIE says could be held before March 2027 if the government calls them.

How people vote

  • Voting is voluntary from 18. Deputies are elected one per constituency in up to two rounds, as individuals: party lists and public campaign funding no longer exist.
  • The President is elected by direct universal suffrage in two rounds; in 2024 Kaïs Saïed won in the first round with 90.69% on a 28.8% turnout.
  • Tunisians abroad elect their own deputies in overseas constituencies and vote at consulates.

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from the President, the government or at least ten deputies; presidential bills have priority.
  2. The ARP debates and votes the text; budget and development-plan laws also need the National Council of Regions and Districts.
  3. The President promulgates the law or sends it back for a second reading; it is published in the Official Journal (JORT).
  4. Short cut: between sessions the President legislates by decree-law, as with the 2022 cybercrime decree-law 54.

Why it's complicated

On 25 July 2021 President Kaïs Saïed froze parliament under an emergency article, then dissolved it, ruled by decree and had a new Constitution adopted by referendum in 2022. The 161 deputies elected in 2022-2023, with 11.22% and 11.4% turnout over the two rounds, ran as individuals, and most opposition parties boycotted. Parliamentary blocs therefore do not match parties: this page groups the President's camp in one card and adds the main parties that still exist legally, even without seats, capped at 7 cards. In the October 2024 presidential election the ISIE refused to reinstate candidates the Administrative Court had ordered back on the ballot, and parliament stripped that court of electoral disputes nine days before the vote. Saïed won 90.69% on a 28.8% turnout. Many opposition leaders are in prison: the 'conspiracy' sentences of up to 45 years became final in September 2026; HRW calls the convictions politically motivated, and the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found the detention of Rached Ghannouchi and Abir Moussi arbitrary.

Why it's like thisHabib Bourguiba led Tunisia from independence in 1956 to 1987 under a single-party presidency, and Zine el-Abidine Ben Ali ruled until the 2011 revolution. The 2014 Constitution, negotiated through a National Dialogue, split power between a directly elected President and a government answerable to parliament; Freedom House then rated Tunisia 'Free' (78/100 in 2017). Fragmented coalitions, a Constitutional Court that was never set up and economic stagnation fed frustration. Kaïs Saïed, a constitutional law lecturer elected in 2019 without a party, argued that the parliamentary system had failed and that power should be rebuilt 'from the bottom up'; the 2022 Constitution reflects that idea, with a strong presidency, individual candidacies and indirectly elected regional councils.

Who's at the wheel

President Kaïs Saïed (no party), re-elected on 6 October 2024; head of government Sara Zaafrani Zenzri, appointed by him on 21 March 2025 after the dismissal of Kamel Maddouri.

Saïed won 90.69% of the votes cast (2,438,954 votes) on a 28.8% turnout, against Ayachi Zammel (7.35%) and Zouhair Maghzaoui (1.97%). Candidates the Administrative Court had ordered reinstated, including Mondher Zenaidi and Abdellatif Mekki, stayed off the ballot. The government is not drawn from a parliamentary majority: it is appointed by the President and answers to him.

What the government promised

  • 2027 finance bill guidelines (4 August 2026), framed by the 2026-2030 development plan: protect low-income households, restructure public enterprises, create jobs for graduates, bring the informal sector into the formal economy
  • Law on employment contracts banning labour subcontracting, published in the Official Journal in May 2025: every contract presumed open-ended
  • Penal reconciliation: business people accused of graft repay money to fund regional projects; the President set a target of 13.5 billion dinars
