5 presidents won the White House with fewer votes nationwide than their opponent.

John Quincy Adams (1824), Rutherford B. Hayes (1876), Benjamin Harrison (1888), George W. Bush (2000) and Donald Trump (2016). The Electoral College counts states, not heads: win a big state by one vote and you take all its electors. In 2024 the winner also had the most votes, which in this system is a pleasant coincidence rather than a rule.

Who decides what

Federal government (Washington, D.C.)Three separate branches. The President, elected for four years (two terms at most), heads the government and the armed forces. Congress (435 representatives, 100 senators) writes laws and the budget. The Supreme Court (nine justices appointed for life) can strike down laws and presidential acts. Washington handles defence, foreign policy, trade, the currency, immigration and big national programmes such as Social Security and Medicare.
The 50 statesFederalism in practice: each state has its own constitution, governor, parliament and courts. States run elections, write most criminal law, run schools, roads and police, and co-manage health care for the poor (Medicaid). The Constitution gives Washington only listed powers; the rest belongs to the states or the people (10th Amendment). The same act can be legal in one state and a crime across the border.
Counties, cities and school districtsTens of thousands of local governments. Voters often elect not only mayors and councils but also sheriffs, local prosecutors, judges and school boards.
Tribal nations, D.C. and territoriesFederally recognised Native American tribes have their own governments. Washington, D.C., Puerto Rico and other territories send only non-voting delegates to the House and have no senators.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is introduced in the House or the Senate (tax bills must start in the House) and sent to a committee, where most bills die quietly.
  2. Both chambers must pass exactly the same text. In the House a simple majority is enough.
  3. In the Senate, the filibuster: any senator can block a final vote, and ending debate ('cloture') takes 60 of 100 votes. So most laws need some votes from the minority party. Budget 'reconciliation' bills escape the filibuster and pass with 51 votes; the 2025 tax law was passed that way.
  4. The President signs or vetoes. Congress can override a veto with two thirds of both chambers.
  5. Courts can then strike down a law or a presidential act. In 2026 the Supreme Court cancelled the President's emergency tariffs because Congress had never authorised them.

Why it's complicated

Power is split and the two parties rarely agree. Funding fights produced the longest shutdown in history, 43 days in October-November 2025, then a 75-day shutdown of the Homeland Security department (14 February-30 April 2026) over immigration enforcement. The President governs largely by executive order, and the Supreme Court has become the referee: in 2026 it struck down his emergency tariffs (6-3) and his order on birthright citizenship (6-3), but allowed him to fire a member of an independent agency, the FTC. US and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February 2026 led to a war, an April ceasefire and a June resolution of Congress, adopted 50-48 in the Senate, directing the withdrawal of forces, which the White House calls unconstitutional. On 3 November 2026, voters handed both chambers of Congress to the Democrats: at least 255 of the 435 House seats, with 13 races still uncalled, and at least 55 of the 100 Senate seats with their two independent allies, after Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith conceded in Mississippi on 11 November; Alaska's Senate race is still being counted (AP, 12 November). From January, every budget and every law will need both parties. The President said that if he had been on the ballot, Republicans would have won.

Why it's like thisThe Constitution of 1787 was written by former colonies that had just fought a king and did not trust any central power. So it divides power three ways (separation of powers) and between Washington and the states (federalism). The Senate, with two seats per state, was the price small states demanded to join; the Electoral College was a compromise between letting Congress pick the President and a direct national vote. The filibuster is not in the Constitution at all: it grew out of the Senate's own rules, which let debate run on unless a supermajority stops it.

Who's at the wheel

President Donald Trump (Republican) since 20 January 2025, with Vice President JD Vance. Republicans control both chambers of Congress until 3 January 2027; Mike Johnson is Speaker of the House. In the 3 November 2026 midterms, Democrats won the House and the Senate (AP race calls): from January, the President will govern with a Congress run by the opposition. Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries has declared his candidacy for Speaker; House Democrats are due to choose their nominee in mid-December. In spring 2026 the President dismissed Attorney General Pam Bondi and Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

There is no national register: you register with your state, and deadlines range from several weeks before the vote to same-day registration, depending on the state. Most states offer early voting or mail ballots, and 36 have voter ID laws. Citizens living abroad and military voters can register and request an absentee ballot with the Federal Post Card Application, supported by the Federal Voting Assistance Program.

Last turnout: 65.3% of the citizen voting-age population in the November 2024 presidential election (about 154 million voters; 73.6% registered) source

The electoral system

  • The President is chosen by the Electoral College: each state has as many electors as it has members of Congress (D.C. has 3), 538 in all, and 270 are needed. In 48 states the candidate who comes first takes all the state's electors; Maine and Nebraska split theirs by district. In 2024 Donald Trump won 312 electors to 226 with 49.8% of the popular vote.
  • The House is elected every two years, one member per district, and whoever gets the most votes wins. Districts are redrawn by the states, so their shape is a political battle in itself. Senators serve six years, a third are elected every two years, and every state has two, whether it is Wyoming or California.
  • Candidates are usually picked in party primaries. Each state sets its own rules for registration, early and mail voting and ID: 36 states have voter ID laws.
Blank vote: what it says, what it changes

In the United States there is no blank ballot as such: a ballot often lists dozens of races, and you can leave any of them empty.

What it is

  • Undervote: you skip a race, on purpose or by mistake. Your other choices on the ballot are still counted. source
  • In Nevada only, since 1975: a “None of these candidates” option in statewide and presidential races. source

How it is counted

  • A skipped race adds to no candidate. The result is decided by the votes given to candidates. source
  • In Nevada, “None of these candidates” can come first but cannot win: the candidate with the most votes is declared the winner. source

The figures

  • 63%for “None of these candidates” in Nevada's 2024 Republican presidential primary, against 30% for Nikki Haley, who was still recorded as the winner. source
  • 1.9%was the highest score of that option in a presidential race in Nevada before 2016. source

What it changes for the result

  • In 1998, “None of these candidates” got 8,125 votes in Nevada's Senate race, decided by 401 votes. Refusals outnumbered the margin twenty times over. source
  • In a first-past-the-post race, the gap between candidates is made only of votes for them: a skipped race leaves the decision to others.

Elsewhere, a blank vote has effects

  • FranceCounted separately and published since 2014, but outside the votes cast: no effect on the result or on thresholds. source
  • SpainBlank votes count as valid votes. They raise the number of votes a list needs to pass the 3% bar for seats. Only an empty envelope is blank: a blank sheet inside is spoilt. source source
  • ColombiaIf blank votes win a majority of valid votes (first presidential round, governors, mayors, assemblies), the election is held again once, without the same candidates. source
  • PeruIf spoilt and blank votes, together or separately, exceed two thirds of votes cast, the election is annulled. source
  • ItalyBlank ballots do not count as valid votes, but in abrogative referendums they count towards the turnout quorum: voting blank helps reach it, abstaining helps defeat it. source
  • IndiaSince a 2013 Supreme Court ruling, voting machines offer “None of the above”. Some states (Maharashtra, Haryana) rerun local elections when that option comes first. source

For lawmakers and citizens who want to change the rule: each model has a cost. Rerunning an election takes time and money, a low threshold can leave seats empty, and counting blank votes changes majorities and thresholds.

Questions to ask yourself

  • If no option represents me, do I want that refusal to be counted? Check above how it is counted here.
  • Do I reject every option, or is one of them less far from my ideas than the others?
  • Do I want to weigh on the result, or to make a refusal count?
  • Is the race close, is the country going through a crisis, could the election tip one way or the other? The closer it is, the more the outcome rests on the votes given to candidates.
  • Blank, spoilt or abstention: what does the law of this country do with each one?
