The rules of the game: who decides what, how people vote, how a law is made.

5 presidents won the White House with fewer votes nationwide than their opponent.

John Quincy Adams (1824), Rutherford B. Hayes (1876), Benjamin Harrison (1888), George W. Bush (2000) and Donald Trump (2016). The Electoral College counts states, not heads: win a big state by one vote and you take all its electors. In 2024 the winner also had the most votes, which in this system is a pleasant coincidence rather than a rule.

Sources: FactCheck.org: presidents who won without the popular vote (1824-2000) · OPB/NPR: 2016 and 2024 popular vote

Who decides what

Federal government (Washington)Three separate branches. The President, elected for four years (two terms at most), leads the government and is commander in chief. Congress (435 representatives, 100 senators) passes laws and the budget. The Supreme Court (nine justices, who serve during good behaviour) can strike down laws and presidential acts.
The 50 statesPowers the Constitution does not give to Washington belong to the states or to the people (10th Amendment). The states set the rules for elections to Congress, unless Congress changes them.
D.C. and territoriesWashington D.C., Puerto Rico and four other territories send delegates to the House who cannot vote in the full House; they have no senators.

Sources: Constitution, Articles I-III (National Constitution Center) · National Archives: Amendment XXII · National Archives: Bill of Rights (10th Amendment) · House of Representatives: the House explained · Supreme Court: the justices

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is introduced in the House or the Senate (tax bills must start in the House) and sent to a committee, where most bills die quietly.
  2. Both chambers must pass exactly the same text. In the House a simple majority is enough.
  3. In the Senate, the filibuster: any senator can block a final vote, and ending debate ('cloture') takes 60 of 100 votes. So most laws need some votes from the minority party. Budget 'reconciliation' bills escape the filibuster and pass with 51 votes; the 2025 tax law was passed that way.
  4. The President signs or vetoes. Congress can override a veto with two thirds of both chambers.
  5. Courts can then strike down a law or a presidential act. In 2026 the Supreme Court cancelled the President's emergency tariffs because Congress had never authorised them.

Sources: Congressional Research Service: introduction to the legislative process · NPR: Senate passes the 2025 tax law by reconciliation, 51-50, Vice President Vance breaking the tie · NPR: Supreme Court strikes down the tariffs (20 February 2026)

Political and economic context

Budget battles caused the longest federal government shutdown in history, 43 days in October-November 2025, then a record shutdown of the Department of Homeland Security over immigration enforcement, which ended on its 76th day, 30 April 2026. The Supreme Court has become the arbiter: in February 2026 it struck down the President's emergency tariffs (6-3), and in June it rejected his order limiting birthright citizenship (6-3). In June 2026 the Senate adopted, by 50 votes to 48, a non-binding resolution calling for US forces to be withdrawn from hostilities with Iran. On 3 November 2026 voters gave both chambers of Congress to the Democrats (AP): every budget and every law will have to get through both parties for the last two years of the term.

Sources: NPR: longest shutdown in US history ends after 43 days · NPR: Congress ends the record DHS shutdown on its 76th day (30 April 2026) · NPR: tariffs struck down (20 February 2026) · University of Washington: birthright citizenship upheld (30 June 2026) · NPR: Senate Iran war powers vote, 50-48 · NPR, from AP race calls: Democrats win full control of Congress

Why it's like thisThe 1787 Constitution divides power three ways (legislative, executive, judicial) and between Washington and the states (10th Amendment). Equal representation of the states in the Senate was the demand of the small states; the electoral college is, in part, a compromise between election of the President by Congress and a popular vote. The filibuster comes from the Senate's right of unlimited debate; since 1975, ending it takes 60 votes.

Sources: Constitution (National Constitution Center) · National Archives: 10th Amendment · Senate: the Great Compromise · National Archives: the electoral college · Senate: filibusters and cloture
