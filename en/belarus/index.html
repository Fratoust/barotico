Country profile · 1 Nov 2026

Belarus

Presidential republic · Eastern Europe, between Russia, Ukraine, Poland and the Baltic states

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Next vote≈ 28 monthsNext national vote: House of Representatives and local councils on a single voting day, expected in February 2029 (date estimated). Elections are held but are not free (Freedom House: 'Not Free', 7/100; OSCE/ODIHR did not observe the 2025 presidential vote, for lack of a timely invitation)
The first 'single voting day' for parliament and local councils was held on 25 February 2024; the five-year term points to February 2029, to be confirmed. Before that, the next formal national step is the third session of the All-Belarusian People's Assembly in Minsk on 17-18 December 2026. The next presidential election is due in 2030: Alexander Lukashenko was re-elected on 26 January 2025 for five years, and the two-term limit introduced in 2022 only counts from the newly elected president's taking office. source

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Citizens from 18 are on the list of their registered address and vote at their polling station with a passport, on election day or during early voting. In 2025 no polling stations opened abroad, so Belarusians living outside the country could not vote (Human Rights Watch).

Last turnout: 85.69% in the January 2025 presidential election and 72.98% in the February 2024 parliamentary election, according to the election commission; neither figure can be checked independently source

Who decides what

PresidentAlexander Lukashenko, in office since 1994, credited with 86.82% in January 2025. Elected for five years; he appoints the government and part of the Senate, and Freedom House says other institutions provide 'no check' on his power.
All-Belarusian People's AssemblySince the 2022 Constitution, the 'highest representative body': up to 1,200 delegates for five years, chaired by Lukashenko. It approves the five-year development programme and policy guidelines, can rule on the legitimacy of elections and can remove the President. It meets at least once a year.
GovernmentThe Council of Ministers, led by Prime Minister Alexander Turchin, is appointed by and answers to the President.
National Assembly (parliament)House of Representatives: 110 deputies, one per district, for five years; since 2024, 51 Belaya Rus, 8 Labour and Justice, 7 Communists, 4 Liberal Democrats and 40 non-partisans. Council of the Republic: 56 senators chosen by regional councils (16 from Belaya Rus) plus 8 appointed by the President.
Local councilsElected on the same single voting day as the House; regional councils in turn elect most of the Senate.

How people vote

  • Voting is voluntary from 18. Each voter elects one deputy in their district, and local councillors the same day; early voting runs in the days before (41.81% voted early in 2025).
  • The President is elected by direct vote for five years; a candidate needs 100,000 signatures and, since 2022, must be at least 40 and have lived in Belarus for 20 years without foreign residence rights.
  • Since October 2023 only four parties are registered; the other eleven were dissolved after a law raised the minimum membership from 1,000 to 5,000.

How a law is passed

  1. The All-Belarusian People's Assembly approves the five-year programme, which officials call 'a law for the further work of all state bodies'.
  2. Bills come mostly from the President and the government; the House of Representatives adopts them.
  3. The Council of the Republic approves or rejects them.
  4. The President signs them or sends them back; he also governs by decree.

Why it's complicated

Belarus holds elections, but Freedom House rates it 'Not Free' (7/100, 1/40 for political rights) and writes that 'elections are openly rigged'. OSCE/ODIHR did not observe the January 2025 presidential election: Minsk sent its invitation too late, and ODIHR said it needed a timely one. Human Rights Watch noted that every 2020 challenger was in prison or exile and that Belarusians abroad could not vote. In 2023 a new party law raised the minimum membership to 5,000 and forced re-registration: four parties out of fifteen survived, all supporting the President. In February 2024 they won 70 of 110 seats and non-partisans the other 40; the turnout of 72.98% given by the election commission cannot be checked independently. The UN Group of Independent Experts on Belarus found in February 2025 that some violations since 2020 amount to crimes against humanity, including 'imprisonment and persecution on political grounds', with hundreds of exiles tried in absentia. In March 2026 the ICC prosecutor opened an investigation, referred by Lithuania, into deportation and persecution on political grounds. Viasna counted more than 1,100 political prisoners at the end of 2025 (Freedom House) and 883 in late July 2026. After talks with the United States, which lifted sanctions on potash among others, Minsk released 123 prisoners in December 2025, among them Nobel laureate Ales Bialiatski, Maria Kalesnikava and Viktar Babaryka, and 250 in March 2026; the US envoy counts more than 500 released with US engagement, while new arrests continue. Party cards: the four registered parties, plus a card for the democratic forces in exile (Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya's office, the United Transitional Cabinet and the Coordination Council, elected online). They are not a party and cannot run in Belarus, where the authorities call them 'extremist'; we show them on the same format because they are the only organised political alternative, and every card follows the same rules.

Why it's like thisBelarus became independent from the Soviet Union in 1991. Alexander Lukashenko won the first presidential election in 1994; a 1996 referendum strengthened the presidency after a clash with parliament, and a 2004 referendum removed the two-term limit. The disputed August 2020 election, whose result the US and the EU do not recognise, was followed by the largest protests in the country's history and a crackdown. A 2022 referendum adopted a new Constitution that restored a two-term limit counted from the next mandate, made the All-Belarusian People's Assembly a constitutional body and gave former presidents immunity for acts linked to their office.

Who's at the wheel

President Alexander Lukashenko and Prime Minister Alexander Turchin's government, backed by a parliament with no opposition: Belaya Rus (51 seats), its three allied parties (19) and 40 non-partisan deputies.

Through the January 2025 presidential election: 86.82% for Lukashenko on an official turnout of 85.69%, with no independent observers. Freedom House says elections are 'openly rigged'; the exile outlet Zerkalo states that no vote since 1994 has been honest or free.

What the government promised

  • Programme of socio-economic development 2026-2030, approved unanimously by the VII All-Belarusian People's Assembly on 19 December 2025: GDP +15.8% and real incomes +21% over five years
  • Previous programme 2021-2025: real GDP +20%, real incomes +20%, exports up by more than $50 billion, inflation of no more than 5% a year
  • 2026 targets: GDP growth of 2.8%; for 2027, 1.9% growth and inflation of about 6%

What independent promise trackers found

  • No independent promise tracker found. Zerkalo compared the 2021-2025 programme with official data: GDP growth came in 'more than twice as modest' as the 20% target (the Prime Minister himself gave 7%), the 5% inflation ceiling was missed every year (15.2% in 2022), and the 20% real-income target was met. source

  • UNDP: Human Development Index 0.824 (2023), 65th in the world, up from 69th; life expectancy 74.4 years. source

If you're considering voting for the democratic forces, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

In 2020 Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya said a vote for her was a vote 'for new and honest elections'. A 2024 protocol made her national leader with no end date, until free elections are held or she steps down.

Its Coordination Council is meant to represent Belarusians. 2,113 people voted for it in May 2026, against 6,723 in 2024.

It thanks Washington for prisoner releases and asks Europe to keep its sanctions: 'American sanctions for the release of people, European sanctions for the liberation of the country'.

To be fair, it has never governed, so no statistics office has measured a result of its own; it is the only force on this page whose leaders face votes they can lose.
