Parliamentary constitutional monarchy · 18.1 million people

How to vote, in practice

Residents are registered automatically: a voting pass arrives by post and is shown with ID at any polling station in your municipality. You may vote by proxy. Dutch citizens abroad must register once with the municipality of The Hague and can then vote by post or at some embassies.

Last turnout: 78.3% in the October 2025 general election (10.64 million of 13.59 million voters) source

Who decides what

European UnionDutch MEPs elected every five years (next in 2029); EU law applies directly.
NationalKing (ceremonial head of state), cabinet led by the prime minister, and the States General: Tweede Kamer (150, directly elected) and Eerste Kamer (75, elected by the provinces).
Provinces (12)Provincial councils handle spatial planning, nature and regional transport, and elect the Eerste Kamer.
Water boardsElected water authorities (waterschappen) manage dikes, water levels and water quality: one of the oldest democratic bodies in the country.
MunicipalitiesElected councils (last elected March 2026) run housing permits, social support and local services; mayors are appointed by the Crown.

How people vote

  • Citizens aged 18+ vote for one candidate on a party list; the whole country works as a single constituency for the 150 seats.
  • No legal threshold: a seat (the kiesdeler) is about 1/150 of valid votes, roughly 0.67% or some 70,000 votes in 2025. Result: 15 parties in parliament.
  • Preference votes count: a candidate with personal votes worth at least a quarter of a seat jumps the party’s list order.
  • The Eerste Kamer (75) is elected indirectly by the provincial councils (plus electoral colleges for the Caribbean Netherlands and Dutch voters abroad) after the provincial elections.

How a law is passed

  1. A ministry drafts a bill (MPs can also table their own); the Raad van State (Council of State) gives advice.
  2. The Tweede Kamer debates, amends and votes: 76 of 150 votes are needed, so a minority cabinet always needs opposition votes.
  3. The Eerste Kamer can only accept or reject the whole bill, not amend it; the coalition holds 22 of its 75 seats.
  4. The King signs, a minister countersigns, and the law is published in the Staatsblad.
  5. Courts may not test acts of parliament against the Constitution, only against binding international treaties.

Why it's complicated

The Jetten cabinet holds 66 of 150 seats and 22 of 75 senators, so every law needs opposition votes: sometimes from the left (PRO, 20 seats and 14 senators), sometimes from the right (JA21, SGP, BBB with 11 senators). The 2027 budget went to parliament without a deal with any opposition party, and the tax plan vote is set for 12 November 2026. The chamber has split into 17 groups, and energy prices linked to the war in Iran weigh on purchasing power.

Why it's like thisSince 1917 the Netherlands has used nationwide proportional representation, part of a settlement that also ended a long dispute over funding religious schools. Society was organised in 'pillars' (Catholic, Protestant, socialist, liberal), none able to rule alone, so coalitions became the rule: no party has held a majority since. The near-absent threshold keeps small parties alive, and a Senate elected by the provinces adds a second check.

Who's at the wheel

Jetten cabinet (since 23 February 2026): minority coalition of D66, VVD and CDA, 66 of 150 seats. Prime minister Rob Jetten (D66); deputy PMs Dilan Yeşilgöz (VVD, Defence) and Bart van den Brink (CDA, Asylum and Migration); 18 ministers and 10 state secretaries.

The Schoof cabinet (PVV, VVD, NSC, BBB) fell on 3 June 2025 when the PVV walked out over asylum. At the snap election of 29 October 2025, D66 and the PVV both won 26 seats, D66 with 29,668 more votes. After 117 days of talks, D66, VVD and CDA chose a minority cabinet on 9 January 2026: the VVD wanted JA21 in (75 seats), D66 refused.

If you’re considering voting for BBB, it’s worth looking beyond the slogans.

It promised ‘BBB delivers’. After a year in government, its voters’ trust in national politics had fallen from 57% to 13% (Ipsos I&O for NOS, September 2025).

On the promise-o-meter: a nitrogen fix the Council of State doubted would hold up in court, about 80,000 homes added against a target of 100,000, and a lower deductible that never arrived.
