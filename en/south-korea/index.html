South Korea

Presidential republic · 51.6 million people (2026)

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

Who decides what

PresidentHead of state and of the executive, elected directly for a single term; appoints the prime minister with the National Assembly’s consent.
National AssemblyA single chamber of 300 members: passes laws and the budget, confirms the prime minister, and can impeach with a two-thirds vote.
Metropolitan cities and provincesElected mayors and governors with their own councils; in June 2026 the Democratic Party won 12 of the 16 races and the People Power Party 4, including Seoul.
Cities, counties and districts227 elected local government heads and councils.

Sources: KBS: results of the 3 June 2026 local and by-elections · YTN: the prime minister's confirmation by the National Assembly

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is tabled by members or the government and examined by a standing committee, then by the Legislation and Judiciary Committee.
  2. A simple majority passes it in plenary; with 180 votes, a bill can be put on the fast track (180 days in committee, 90 in the Judiciary Committee) and a filibuster ended.
  3. The president can send a law back; overriding that veto takes two thirds of the members, as do constitutional amendments and impeachment.

Sources: YTN: majorities that matter in the National Assembly (151, 180, 200, 20) · SBS: two thirds needed for constitutional amendments, impeachment and veto overrides · Bill no. 2200165: fast-track review periods (180 + 90 days)

Political and economic context

The Democratic Party holds 161 of 300 seats and 179 with its allies, enough to pass laws and end filibusters but short of the 200 needed to amend the Constitution. Presidential approval fell from above 50% to 37–41% in autumn 2026, real estate being the top complaint. The courts have suspended the president’s own trials under the Constitution, which the opposition contests, while his predecessor is being tried for insurrection.

Sources: SBS: seats after the June 2026 by-elections · SBS: Gallup Korea, 3-5 November 2026 · YTN: debate on the president's suspended trials (16 October 2026)

Why it's like thisThe 1987 Constitution came out of the June 1987 pro-democracy movement for a directly elected president. The ruling camp wanted a single six-year term, the opposition a four-year term with re-election; the compromise, approved by referendum on 29 October 1987, was a single five-year term, with the president's emergency powers and power to dissolve the Assembly abolished. A 2026 debate on allowing one re-election reopens that compromise.

Sources: National Archives of Korea: the 9th constitutional amendment (1987) · YTN: debate on a constitutional amendment allowing re-election (September 2026) · Freedom House 2026: South Korea

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Four parties have a card: those with a parliamentary group or at least 3% of the vote in the 2024 election. The Progressive, Basic Income and Social Democratic parties (six seats in all) appear only in the seat chart. Seats come from the June 2026 by-election count, adjusted for one seat lost in July 2026; the eight independents are worked out from the 300 total, because the National Assembly's own page could not be opened.

Left-right positions are an editorial estimate: the Chapel Hill expert survey does not cover South Korea. Press sources are limited to Yonhap, the national news agency, and to the five broadcasters with the highest trust scores in the Reuters Institute report (MBC, JTBC, SBS, YTN, KBS).

Party finance shares, programme links (except the Reform Party's) and official 2024 vote counts are missing: the National Election Commission's accounts and results pages could not be reached. The homicide rate is worked out from police victim counts. No independent promise tracker or poll on parties people would never vote for was found.

The court record lists only official acts. Cho Kuk's conviction and pardon are left out until an official text can be opened.
