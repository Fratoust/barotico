Portugal

Semi-presidential parliamentary republic · 11.4 million people

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

4 trips to the polls in under 9 months: legislative (May 2025), local (October 2025), presidential round one (January 2026) and round two (February 2026).

The May 2025 vote was also the third legislative election in just over three years. In May 2026 the Prime Minister summed up the national mood: people are "fed up with elections".

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Citizens are registered automatically from 18. Portuguese living abroad elect 4 of the 230 deputies through two overseas constituencies. Practical details (polling station, early voting) are published by the Comissão Nacional de Eleições before each vote.

Last turnout: 58.3% in the May 2025 legislative election (including voters abroad); 50.0% in the February 2026 presidential run-off source

Who decides what

NationalPresident (elected, 5 years, can dissolve parliament), Assembly of the Republic (230 deputies) and the Government led by the Prime Minister.
Autonomous regionsAzores and Madeira have their own elected assemblies and regional governments.
Municipalities308 municipal councils (câmaras) elected every four years; last vote 12 October 2025.
ParishesFreguesias, the smallest elected tier, voted on the same day as the municipalities.

How people vote

  • Assembly: 230 deputies by proportional representation (D'Hondt) on closed lists in 22 constituencies, including two for Portuguese living abroad.
  • President: direct vote in two rounds, five-year term, at most two in a row.
  • No national threshold: a party can win a seat with well under 1% of the national vote if it is concentrated, as JPP did in Madeira in 2025.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is tabled by the Government, deputies, parliamentary groups, regional assemblies or citizens.
  2. Parliament votes on the principle, a committee works on the detail, then a final overall vote.
  3. The President signs it, vetoes it, or sends it to the Constitutional Court for review first.
  4. Parliament can override a veto by absolute majority (two-thirds for some laws); the law is published in the Diário da República.

Why it's complicated

The AD government holds 91 of 230 seats, far from the 116 needed for a majority, and must find votes bill by bill on its left (PS) or its right (Chega, IL). The right as a whole has enough seats to revise the Constitution without the Socialists. Polls in July 2026 put PS and AD level at 29% each with Chega at 21%.

Why it's like thisAfter 48 years of dictatorship ended by the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974, the 1976 Constitution spread power between an elected President, parliament and government, and chose proportional voting so that no single force could dominate again.

Who's at the wheel

XXV Constitutional Government (PSD/CDS-PP, "AD"), Prime Minister Luís Montenegro, sworn in on 5 June 2025. President: António José Seguro, elected on 8 February 2026 with 66.8%.

Minority government. Montenegro's first cabinet fell on a lost confidence vote on 11 March 2025 over his family firm Spinumviva; the snap election gave AD more seats but no majority. Its budgets pass thanks to PS abstentions (2026 and 2027), while its labour reform was voted down by PS, Chega and the left on 19 June 2026.

If you're considering voting for the PSD, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

In 2024 its government promised a family doctor for everyone by the end of 2025. At the end of that year, 1,563,710 people still had none, about as many as when it took office.

It promises lower taxes on work. Its 2025 income-tax cut was worth about €500 million; the tax burden, meanwhile, rose to 35.4% of GDP. Its state guarantee helped 25,519 young people get a mortgage, as house prices jumped 17.6%.

Its foreigners and nationality laws, passed with Chega, were partly struck down by the Constitutional Court in 2025 and 2026, on family protection, equality and proportionality.

To be fair, it has run budget surpluses, debt fell to 89.7% of GDP, and unemployment fell to 6.0% in 2025, the lowest since 2011 (INE).
