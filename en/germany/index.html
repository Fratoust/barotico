Germany

Federal parliamentary republic · 83.5 million people · 16 Länder

The systemThe governmentThe partiesVotingKey figuresWatch listMedia

2 secret ballots on the same day were needed to elect Friedrich Merz chancellor in May 2025, a first in the Federal Republic's history.

In the first round he got 310 votes, 6 short of the 316 needed, although CDU/CSU and SPD hold 328 seats. The ballot is secret, so nobody knows who said no. He won in a second round that afternoon.

Who decides what

Federal (Bund)The Bundestag (630 MPs) makes federal law and elects the chancellor; the Bundesrat represents the 16 state governments; the Federal President is head of state with a mostly ceremonial role.
States (Länder)16 Länder with their own constitution, parliament and government. They run schools, universities, police and culture, and carry out most federal laws.
Districts and municipalitiesDistricts, independent cities and municipalities handle local services, social assistance, building permits and local roads, with elected councils and mayors.
European UnionGermany sends the largest national delegation to the European Parliament and shares the rules on trade, competition and the single market with the other EU states.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill comes from the federal government, a group of MPs or the Bundesrat. Government bills first go to the Bundesrat for comment.
  2. The Bundestag debates it in three readings, with the detailed work done in committees, then votes.
  3. The Bundesrat must approve laws that touch the Länder; for other laws it can only object, and the Bundestag can override it. A joint mediation committee looks for compromises.
  4. The Federal President signs and publishes the law. Changing the Basic Law needs two thirds in both chambers, as for the March 2025 debt-brake reform.
  5. The Federal Constitutional Court in Karlsruhe can strike a law down, for example the 2021 supplementary budget in 2023.

Why it's complicated

The Merz coalition (CDU/CSU and SPD) holds 328 of 630 seats, just 12 more than the 316 needed for a majority. Changing the Basic Law needs 420 votes: the coalition plus the Greens reaches only 413, so the Left or the AfD would be needed, and the other parties rule out working with the AfD. In 2026 the AfD came first in Saxony-Anhalt (43.8%) and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (38.2%), the Left came first in Berlin and the CDU dropped out of the Mecklenburg-Vorpommern parliament. A court has suspended, pending the main case, the domestic intelligence service's label of the AfD as a confirmed right-wing extremist party; it remains a suspected case. In October 2026 only 10% were satisfied with the government.

Why it's like thisThe 1949 Basic Law was written against the memory of the Weimar Republic's collapse and the Nazi dictatorship. Hence a chancellor who can only be removed if parliament elects a successor at the same time, a largely ceremonial president, a 5% threshold against fragmentation, strong Länder that the Allies wanted as a check on central power, a powerful Constitutional Court, and a 'defensive democracy' in which the court can ban parties that fight the constitutional order. In 1990 the eastern Länder joined this system.

Who governs in Germany?

Who's at the wheel

Chancellor Friedrich Merz (CDU) leads a CDU/CSU-SPD coalition since May 2025, with Lars Klingbeil (SPD) as vice-chancellor and finance minister. Labour minister Bärbel Bas (SPD) resigned in October 2026; Alexander Schweitzer (SPD) is to succeed her.

The Bundestag elects the chancellor on the Federal President's proposal; the chancellor picks the ministers. It can only be replaced by a 'constructive' no-confidence vote that elects a successor.

Voting in Germany

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

German citizens aged 18+ receive a polling notice by post. A postal ballot can be requested until 3 pm on the Friday before the vote and must arrive by 6 pm on election day. Germans living abroad must apply to be entered on the electoral roll of their municipality, at the latest on the 21st day before the election.

Last turnout: 82.5% in the February 2025 federal election (76.4% in 2021) source

The electoral system

  • Two votes: the first elects a constituency MP, the second picks a party list and decides each party's share of the 630 seats.
  • Since the 2023 reform the Bundestag is capped at 630 seats: a constituency winner only gets a seat if the party's second-vote result covers it.
  • A party needs 5% of second votes or 3 constituency wins to enter. Parties of national minorities, like the Danish-Frisian SSW (1 seat), are exempt. In 2025 the BSW missed the 5% mark by 9,529 votes.
  • Germans aged 18+ vote for the Bundestag. Postal voting is open to everyone on request.
