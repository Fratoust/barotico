Germany

Federal parliamentary republic · 83.5 million people · 16 Länder

Under Article 39 of the Basic Law the election must take place 46 to 48 months after the current Bundestag first met (25 March 2025): between 26 January and 25 March 2029, on a Sunday or public holiday. The Federal President sets the date. An early election needs a lost confidence vote and a dissolution, as in December 2024. source

The systemThe governmentThe partiesVotingKey figuresMedia

2 secret ballots on the same day were needed to elect Friedrich Merz chancellor in May 2025, a first in the Federal Republic's history.

In the first round he got 310 votes, 6 short of the 316 needed, although the CDU/CSU and SPD hold 328 seats. The vote is secret. He was elected in a second round the same day, with 325 votes.

Sources: Bundestag: chancellor election, 6 May 2025

Who decides what

Federal (Bund)The Bundestag (630 members) passes federal laws and elects the Chancellor; the Bundesrat is made up of representatives of the 16 state governments; the Federal President has a mainly representative role.
States (Länder)16 Länder, each with its own constitution. They alone legislate on schools, universities, culture and police, and they carry out most federal laws.
Districts and municipalities401 districts and district-free cities and 11,054 municipalities, whose self-government is guaranteed by the Basic Law: schools, town planning, social welfare, local transport, waste.

Sources: Federal Returning Officer: two votes, 630 seats · Federal Agency for Civic Education: 75 years of the Basic Law · European Committee of the Regions: Länder, districts, municipalities

How a law is passed

  1. A bill comes from the federal government, members of the Bundestag or the Bundesrat. Government bills go to the Bundesrat first, which has about six weeks to give its opinion.
  2. The Bundestag debates it in three readings, with the detailed work done in committees, then votes.
  3. The Bundesrat must approve the laws that require its consent; for the others it can object, and the Bundestag can then override it by an absolute majority. A mediation committee looks for compromises.
  4. The Federal President checks the law, signs it and publishes it. Amending the Basic Law requires two-thirds of the members of the Bundestag and two-thirds of the votes in the Bundesrat, as for the debt-brake reform of March 2025.
  5. The Federal Constitutional Court in Karlsruhe can strike a law down, for example the 2021 supplementary budget in 2023.

Sources: Bundestag: how a law is made · Basic Law, Art. 79 · Bundestag: Basic Law amendments passed, 18 March 2025 · Federal Constitutional Court: 2021 supplementary budget void (15 November 2023)

Political and economic context

The Merz coalition (CDU/CSU and SPD) holds 328 of the 630 seats, 12 more than the 316 needed. Amending the Basic Law takes 420 votes in the Bundestag: the coalition plus the Greens (85) have only 413, so votes from the Left (64) or the AfD (150 in its group since March 2026) are needed. In 2026 the AfD came first in Saxony-Anhalt (43.8%) and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania (38.2%), the Left came first in Berlin (25.7%), and the CDU dropped out of the Mecklenburg parliament (4.9%).

Sources: Bundestag: coalition seats and chancellor election · Federal Returning Officer: final 2025 result · Basic Law, Art. 79 · Bundestag: Jan Wenzel Schmidt, non-attached since March 2026 · ZDF: Saxony-Anhalt election, 6 September 2026 · ZDF: Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania election, 20 September 2026 · Federal Agency for Civic Education: Berlin election, 20 September 2026

Why it's like thisThe 1949 Basic Law draws lessons from the Weimar Constitution: a Federal President with a mainly representative role, much stronger federalism, a Constitutional Court that guards the whole Constitution and can ban anti-constitutional parties, and a Chancellor who can only be removed by electing a successor.

Sources: Federal Agency for Civic Education: 75 years of the Basic Law · Bundestag: election and term of the Chancellor

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

This page has seven party cards: the five groups in the Bundestag plus the BSW and the FDP, which won more than 3% in February 2025 but no seats. The SSW, a minority party with one seat, has no card. Seats are the official 2025 result; the seat chart in 'Who holds power' is derived from it, minus two MPs whom the Bundestag lists as non-attached, because the Bundestag's seat page could not be opened.

Left-right positions are our estimate of the Chapel Hill Expert Survey 2024, which could not be opened. The AfD is labelled far right by position; Die Linke is labelled far left by an editorial choice following The PopuList. The homicide rate is calculated from Eurostat's count and Destatis's population; the corruption score comes via ZDF.

Party finance shows only total income and the share of state funds: the Bundestag summaries give no further breakdown. Programme links are missing for the five parties outside government. No independent promise tracker was found; ZDF's one-year review is used instead.

Press links come only from the validated list in the media panel (dpa and epd, CORRECTIV, ARD and ZDF, regional dailies), chosen on the 2025 Reuters Institute trust figures. Legal-news sites and other outlets were replaced by court, parliament or government sources, or dropped: one 2026 acquittal and a former MP's first-instance conviction left the page for that reason. Cases without an official act and private-life matters, including the stated reason for a 2026 resignation, are left out.
