Voting in Germany

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

German citizens aged 18+ receive a polling notice by post. A postal ballot can be requested until 3 pm on the Friday before the vote and must arrive by 6 pm on election day. Germans living abroad must apply to be entered on the electoral roll of their municipality, at the latest on the 21st day before the election.

Last turnout: 82.5% in the February 2025 federal election (76.4% in 2021) source

The electoral system

  • Two votes: the first elects a constituency MP, the second picks a party list and decides each party's share of the 630 seats.
  • Since the 2023 reform the Bundestag is capped at 630 seats: a constituency winner only gets a seat if the party's second-vote result covers it.
  • A party needs 5% of second votes or 3 constituencies to enter. National minority parties, such as the Danish-Frisian SSW (1 seat), are exempt. In 2025 the BSW stayed just below, at 4.981%.
  • Germans aged 18 and over vote for the Bundestag. Postal voting is available on request.

Sources: Federal Returning Officer: the two votes · Federal Returning Officer: final 2025 result · Federal Agency for Civic Education: Bundestag elections

Blank vote: what it says, what it changes

In Germany, there is no separate blank vote: a ballot left empty is an invalid vote, counted with the other invalid votes.

What it is

  • Each voter has two votes on the same ballot: a first vote for a local candidate and a second vote for a party list. source
  • A ballot with no mark at all, or not officially issued, makes both votes invalid. So does a ballot whose intention is unclear, or that carries an addition or a reservation. source

How it is counted

  • Invalid votes are published but go to no party: seats are shared out among valid votes. source

The figures

  • 0.6%of second votes were invalid in February 2025 (0.9% in 2021), with a turnout of 82.5%. source
  • 0.8%of first votes were invalid in 2025 (1.0% in 2021). source

What it changes for the result

  • Because a blank ballot is simply invalid, an empty ballot and a spoilt one carry the same weight in the results: none. source

Questions to ask yourself

  • If no option represents me, do I want that refusal to be counted? Check above how it is counted here.
  • Do I reject every option, or is one of them less far from my ideas than the others?
  • Do I want to weigh on the result, or to make a refusal count?
  • Is the race close, is the country going through a crisis, could the election tip one way or the other? The closer it is, the more the outcome rests on the votes given to candidates.
  • Blank, spoilt or abstention: what does the law of this country do with each one?

Barotico does not recommend voting blank, nor avoiding it. These questions are there to help you decide with the facts in hand.

Elsewhere, a blank vote has effects

  • FranceCounted separately and published since 2014, but outside the votes cast: no effect on the result or on thresholds. source
  • SpainBlank votes count as valid votes. They raise the number of votes a list needs to pass the 3% bar for seats. Only an empty envelope is blank: a blank sheet inside is spoilt. source source
  • ColombiaIf blank votes win a majority of valid votes (first presidential round, governors, mayors, assemblies), the election is held again once, without the same candidates. source
  • PeruIf spoilt and blank votes, together or separately, exceed two thirds of votes cast, the election is annulled. source
  • ItalyBlank ballots do not count as valid votes, but in abrogative referendums they count towards the turnout quorum: voting blank helps reach it, abstaining helps defeat it. source
  • Nevada (United States)Since 1975, ballots offer “None of these candidates” in statewide and presidential races. It can come first, but it never wins: the candidate with the most votes is elected. source
  • IndiaSince a 2013 Supreme Court ruling, voting machines offer “None of the above”. It cannot cancel an election: the candidate with the most votes still wins. source

For lawmakers and citizens who want to change the rule: each model has a cost. Rerunning an election takes time and money, a low threshold can leave seats empty, and counting blank votes changes majorities and thresholds.

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

This page has seven party cards: the five groups in the Bundestag plus the BSW and the FDP, which won more than 3% in February 2025 but no seats. The SSW, a minority party with one seat, has no card. Seats are the official 2025 result; the seat chart in 'Who holds power' is derived from it, minus two MPs whom the Bundestag lists as non-attached, because the Bundestag's seat page could not be opened.

Left-right positions are our estimate of the Chapel Hill Expert Survey 2024, which could not be opened. The AfD is labelled far right by position; Die Linke is labelled far left by an editorial choice following The PopuList. The homicide rate is calculated from Eurostat's count and Destatis's population; the corruption score comes via ZDF.

Party finance shows only total income and the share of state funds: the Bundestag summaries give no further breakdown. Programme links are missing for the five parties outside government. No independent promise tracker was found; ZDF's one-year review is used instead.

Press links come only from the validated list in the media panel (dpa and epd, CORRECTIV, ARD and ZDF, regional dailies), chosen on the 2025 Reuters Institute trust figures. Legal-news sites and other outlets were replaced by court, parliament or government sources, or dropped: one 2026 acquittal and a former MP's first-instance conviction left the page for that reason. Cases without an official act and private-life matters, including the stated reason for a 2026 resignation, are left out.
