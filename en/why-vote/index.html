Why vote

Why participation matters — and what abstention changes

Voting is one way to influence who represents you. The practical effect of voting, abstaining or casting a blank ballot depends on the country’s electoral rules. This page explains those effects and presents the case for taking part.

A right that was won, not given

  • 1931Spain: the Constitution gives women the vote, after Clara Campoamor's fight in Parliament. Source: Universidad Autónoma de Madrid
  • 1944France: women win the right to vote, a century and a half after the Revolution. Source: Assemblée nationale
  • 1948Belgium: women's vote in parliamentary elections is passed on 27 March. Source: BX1
  • 1965United States: after the Selma marches, more than 25,000 people reach Montgomery; the Voting Rights Act is signed five months later. Source: U.S. National Archives

Even today, only 26 of 167 countries are rated full democracies by the EIU. Source: EIU Democracy Index 2025

Blank vote: yes, no, and what it changes

Every country profile has a “Voting” tab: how to vote in practice, the electoral system, turnout and, as we roll it out, what a blank vote changes. The rules differ a lot from one country to the next:

  • FranceCounted separately and published, but with no effect on the result or on thresholds. Source: Eurojuris
  • SpainCounted as a valid vote: it raises the 3% bar that gives access to seats. Only an empty envelope is blank. Source: BOE · LOREG
  • ColombiaIf it wins a majority, the election is held again, without the same candidates. Source: Constitución de Colombia, art. 258
  • PeruAbove two thirds of blank or spoilt votes, the election is annulled. Source: Constitución del Perú (1993)

Blank or spoilt? A word or a crossing-out on the paper usually makes the vote spoilt. Check your country's rules before voting.
