How the algorithm locks you in

There is no magic threshold of 10 videos. A platform locks onto your tastes in one to two hours, about 200 videos. It mostly watches how long you linger, far more than your likes.

The vicious circle fits in one sentence: you stop on what shocks you, the algorithm serves more of it, you stop again. Lingering is voting.

It changes how we see each other: in a Stanford study of 1,256 X users, pushing hostile posts down the feed for one week reduced hostility towards the other side, with no difference between left and right.

A reset is no magic wand: in Romania, brand-new accounts that followed only the official candidates were still flooded with political content. Hence the value, now and then, of the non-personalised feed that EU law requires large platforms to offer.

Who speaks, who pays?

The question applies to everyone: a TV channel, a radio station, a newspaper or an influencer. What counts is who owns the outlet, who funds it, and whether it can criticise those who fund it. No type is good or bad by nature.

Type of outletWhat to knowThe question to ask
Public broadcaster in a democracyFunded by public money, but its independence is protected by law. In the EU, since August 2025, states must secure its funding and appoint its management transparently.Can it criticise the government in office, and does it?
State media in an authoritarian regimeControlled by those in power, it carries the regime’s message, including when it speaks abroad in our language.Has it ever criticised its own country’s leaders?
Private mediaOwned by a company or a shareholder whose interests can weigh on the editorial line. In the EU, media must now disclose who owns them.Who owns it, and what other businesses does that owner have?
Reader-funded independent mediaLives on subscriptions or donations; depends less on a shareholder, but often speaks to an audience already convinced.Does it sometimes tell its readers what they don’t want to hear?
Influencer or creatorNo newsroom, no ethics charter: lives on partnerships, donations or platform advertising. In France, any paid promotion must say “Advertising” or “Commercial collaboration”.Who pays them, and do they show it?

7 questions to ask about an account

An account that claims something is not proof. Since October 2025, Meta and Google no longer accept political ads in the EU: political messaging online goes even more through algorithms and creators.

How it works: the mechanisms

Seven mechanisms to read any outlet or influencer. Open each card: it ends with the question to ask.

01Public media: who pays, and what for

It is funded by everyone’s money: a tax or a licence fee depending on the country. In France, the licence fee was abolished in 2022 and replaced by a share of VAT, a system made permanent in December 2024. In return, it must offer impartial information and a plurality of views, for every audience. Criticising it is normal and healthy: it is our money.

The question to askDoes it meet its pluralism obligations? And those who want to cut or abolish it: what do they propose instead?

Source
02When one outlet criticises another

Criticism between media is part of the debate. But an outlet attacking a rival is never a neutral referee: they compete for the same audience and often the same advertisers. When a private group denounces the public broadcaster, or the other way round, ask who would gain. The warning sign is a repeated campaign that serves the owner’s business or political interests.

The question to askDoes the critic have a stake: audience, advertising, its owner’s political project?

03Independent, opinion, partisan: three different things

Independent: no shareholder or government can impose its editorial choices (ethics charter, journalists’ association, diversified funding). Opinion: it openly holds a line, left, right or elsewhere; that is an old democratic tradition, as long as it is stated. Partisan: it serves a party or a cause, sometimes while presenting itself as neutral news. Having a line is never the problem: hiding it is.

The question to askDoes this outlet say clearly where it stands?

04The owner and the group

Media are expensive and often earn little. When one person or group buys several, it is not always for the money: it is also influence. A group whose titles keep varied lines is not a problem. The warning sign is when all the outlets of the same owner push in the same political direction, whichever it is. In the EU, media must now disclose who owns them, and major concentrations are assessed for their impact on pluralism.

The question to askDo the owner’s other outlets all say the same thing?

Source
05How influencers make a living

Five main sources: a share of the platform’s advertising (on YouTube, 55% for long videos and 45% for Shorts), brand partnerships, affiliate links, subscriptions, donations and live gifts, and selling their own products. Their income depends on your attention: the more a video provokes a reaction, the more it pays. And sometimes a political message is paid for without being declared, as in Romania.

The question to askIf this content makes me angry, who profits?

Source
06Groups, channels and chats on social media

A Facebook group, a Telegram channel or a WhatsApp chat can look like “ordinary citizens” and be run by activists or a party. That is not forbidden, but it should be clear. In Romania, the campaign also relied on volunteers coordinated through social media, alongside inauthentic accounts.

The question to askWho runs this group, and do they say so?

Source
07Who is invited, and at what time

In France, TV and radio channels report every month to the regulator, Arcom, which politicians they hosted and for how long; Arcom publishes a review every quarter. Since 2024, pluralism is also assessed through columnists, hosts and guests. Timing matters: a channel was formally warned for airing some parties’ speaking time at night while they were under-represented in the daytime. Many countries have a broadcasting regulator: look up what it publishes.

The question to askWhich parties does my favourite outlet invite most at peak times?

The crossed news diet, in one week

The aim is not to change your mind, but to know what the other side thinks, and why.

  1. Map your diet: list your 5 main sources (TV, radio, newspapers, accounts you follow).
  2. Add two sources: one from the other side, or failing that more central than your habits, and one factual source (news agency, public broadcaster of a neighbouring country).
  3. One topic, three treatments: each day, compare one topic with 5 questions. What is fact, what is opinion? Who is invited to speak? What is missing? What emotion is it trying to trigger? Who owns this outlet?
  4. One conversation: 20 minutes with someone who thinks differently. Listen without contradicting, then restate their view until they say “yes, that’s it”.

You mostly follow outlets from one side: add one from the opposite side.

The other side is too hard to take at first: start with an outlet more central than your habits.

You get your news mainly from political accounts on social media: add a factual source, such as a news agency.

Elsewhere in the world

Wherever manipulation retreated, citizens combined three things: checking together, talking across divides, and demanding rules.

Romania16 NGOs alerted Brussels about TikTok, fact-checkers reported misleading posts in real time, and turnout hit a record in the 2025 re-run, especially among young people.Source
TaiwanCofacts, a citizen bot in the LINE messaging app: forward a dubious message, volunteers answer. Over 2,000 contributors. Motto: humour over rumour.Source
FinlandMedia education from early childhood, a national policy since 2013. First in Europe’s media literacy index every year since 2017.Source
MoldovaFor the September 2025 election, more than 30 organisations monitored and raised alerts in real time. Exposing manipulation quickly defuses it.Source
Germany“Deutschland spricht” pairs strangers with opposite views for a face-to-face talk. A single conversation reduces hostility; among like-minded people, views harden.Source
NetherlandsThe NGO Bits of Freedom won against Meta in October 2025. Since January 2026, Dutch users can keep a non-profiled feed on Instagram and Facebook; Meta has appealed.Source
BulgariaIn December 2025, a mobilisation born on TikTok and Instagram brought over 100,000 people to Sofia. The same platforms can serve civic life when we take back control.Source
