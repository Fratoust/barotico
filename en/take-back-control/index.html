How recommendations can lock you in

Your feed learns from what you watch, and for how long. It is not just a game: in Romania in 2024, a presidential election was cancelled. Intelligence services had spotted a paid online campaign that was kept hidden. So, in front of every video, ask: who is speaking, and who is paying?

~200videos: roughly what it took TikTok to learn the tastes of test accounts. Time spent on a video counts a lot.TikTok audit, 2025
11 %of unwanted videos really go away with YouTube’s “Not interested” button. That is not much.Mozilla, 2022
100+influencers were paid in Romania to push a candidate, without saying it was political advertising.RFE/RL, from declassified Romanian documents
Read more: how your feed adapts to you

There is no magic threshold of 10 videos. Each platform combines several signals and changes their weight over time. On TikTok, the company itself says that whether you finish watching a video is a strong indicator of interest, and an independent audit found the feed locking onto test accounts’ interests within roughly the first 200 videos.

The vicious circle: you stop on what grabs you, often what shocks you; the platform reads it as interest and suggests more of it. Lingering counts as a signal.

It changes how we see each other: in a Stanford study of 1,256 X users, pushing hostile posts down the feed for one week reduced hostility towards the other side, with no difference between left and right.

A reset is no magic wand: in Romania, brand-new accounts that followed only the official candidates were still flooded with political content. Hence the value, now and then, of the non-personalised feed that the EU Digital Services Act requires of very large platforms.

Three reflexes before sharing

  1. Who gains if I see this?
  2. Is it labelled “Sponsored” or “partnership”?
  3. It makes me angry or afraid: why now?

How it works: the mechanisms

Seven simple cases. Open a card: it ends with a question to ask yourself.

01Public media: who pays, and what for

They are paid for with everyone’s money: through a tax or a fee, depending on the country. In return, they must inform everyone, without taking sides, and give a voice to different views. You can criticise them: it is our money.

The question to askDoes it give a voice to different views? And those who want to shut it down, what do they offer instead?

Source: Encyclopaedia Britannica
02When one outlet criticises another

Outlets compete for the same audience and the same advertisers. So when one attacks a rival, it is not a neutral referee. Be most careful with repeated attacks that suit its owner.

The question to askDoes the one attacking have something to gain?

03Independent, opinion, partisan: three different things

An independent outlet: no one can force what it publishes. An opinion outlet: it says openly whether it leans left, right or elsewhere. That is normal. A partisan outlet: it works for a party, sometimes while pretending to be neutral. Having a view is not the problem. Hiding it is.

The question to askDoes this outlet say clearly which way it leans?

04The owner and the group

A media outlet costs a lot and earns little. When someone buys several, it is often also to weigh on ideas. The warning sign: all their outlets say the same thing. In the US in 2018, dozens of local TV presenters read the same text, word for word, at their owner’s request.

The question to askDo the owner’s other outlets all say the same thing?

Source: NPR
05How influencers make a living

From platform ads, brand deals, shopping links, live donations and their own products. The more a video makes people react, the more it earns. In many countries, paid content must be labelled. It is not always done, as in Romania.

The question to askIf this video makes me angry, who makes money?

Source: YouTube Official Blog
06Groups, channels and chats on social media

A Facebook group, a Telegram channel or a WhatsApp group can look like it is run by people like you. Sometimes it is run by activists or a party. That is allowed, but it should be said.

The question to askWho runs this group, and do they say so?

Source: VSquare
07Who is invited, and at what time

In many countries, each party’s speaking time on TV is counted. In France, channels report it every month. The time of day matters too: 3 a.m. is not the evening news.

The question to askWhich parties does my favourite channel invite most at peak times?

Source: Instituto Nacional Electoral (México)

Who is speaking, who is paying: media types and the 7 questions to ask

The question applies to everyone: a TV channel, a radio station, a newspaper or an influencer. What counts is who owns the outlet, who funds it, and whether it can criticise those who fund it. No type is good or bad by nature.

Type of outletWhat to knowThe question to ask
Public broadcaster in a democracyFunded by public money, but its independence is protected by law: how its management is appointed and how it is funded are the key points. In the EU, these guarantees were strengthened in 2025.Can it criticise the government in office, and does it?
State media in an authoritarian regimeControlled by those in power, it carries the regime’s message, including when it speaks abroad in our language.Has it ever criticised its own country’s leaders?
Private mediaOwned by a company or a shareholder whose interests can weigh on the editorial line. More and more countries require media to disclose who owns them.Who owns it, and what other businesses does that owner have?
Reader-funded independent mediaLives on subscriptions or donations; depends less on a shareholder, but often speaks to an audience already convinced.Does it sometimes tell its readers what they don’t want to hear?
Influencer or creatorNo newsroom, no ethics charter: lives on partnerships, donations or platform advertising. In India (since 2021) and France (since 2023), for example, paid promotion must be labelled (“Ad”, “Sponsored”, “Advertising”…).Who pays them, and do they show it?

7 questions to ask about an account

An account that claims something is not proof. And where political ads are restricted (Meta and Google stopped them in the EU in October 2025, for example), political messaging moves even more to algorithms and creators.

QuestionWhere to lookWarning sign
Who is behind it?Bio, website, real name, legal noticeNo name, no link, generic photo
Since when has it existed?“About this account” on Instagram and X: creation dateCreated a few weeks before an election: in Romania, 25,000 TikTok accounts woke up 2 weeks before the vote
Where does it post from?“About this account”: country shown (Instagram for some accounts, X since Nov. 2025)A country very different from the one it claims to defend (beware: a VPN can distort it)
Has it changed its name?Number of username changesSeveral changes: the account may have been bought or recycled
Is it paid?“Advertising” or “Commercial collaboration” labelA sudden political message from an account that used to talk about something else
Who says exactly the same thing?Search the sentence or the hashtagSame words, same hashtag, at the same time, on several accounts
Do the numbers add up?Followers, age of the account, commentsMany followers for a recent account; bursts of identical comments

No single sign proves anything; it’s the accumulation that should alert you.

The crossed news diet, in one week

The goal is not to change your mind. It is to see how others tell the same story, and to check what matters.

  1. Write down your 5 main news sources: channels, sites, accounts you follow.
  2. Add two: one from the other side (or closer to the centre), and a news agency that just gives the facts.
  3. Each day, compare one story across three sources. What is a fact, what is an opinion? What is missing? What emotion are they trying to make you feel?
  4. Talk for 20 minutes with someone who thinks differently. Listen without interrupting, then sum up their view until they say “yes, that’s it”.

You mostly follow one side? Add an outlet from the other side.

The other side annoys you too much? Start with one closer to the centre.

You mostly get news from political accounts? Add a news agency.

Elsewhere in the world: what worked

Wherever manipulation retreated, citizens combined three things: checking together, talking across divides, and demanding rules.

Romania16 NGOs alerted the authorities about TikTok, fact-checkers flagged misleading posts in real time, and turnout hit a record in the 2025 re-run, especially among young people.Source: European Economic and Social Committee
IndiaAfter rumours spread on WhatsApp were linked to lynchings in 2018, WhatsApp capped message forwarding, then extended the cap worldwide. In 2020, a stricter limit cut the spread of viral “highly forwarded” messages by 70%.Source: TechCrunch
BrazilFor the 2018 elections, 24 rival newsrooms joined forces in Comprova and opened a WhatsApp number where anyone could send a rumour to be checked.Source: Nieman Lab
MexicoVerificado 2018 brought together more than 90 media outlets, universities and civic groups from 28 of the country’s 32 states to debunk rumours and fact-check debates live, often with humour.Source: Online News Association
PhilippinesFor the 2022 elections, more than 140 newsrooms, churches, businesses, lawyers and universities formed #FactsFirstPH and produced over 1,500 fact-checks, then spread them in local languages.Source: Meedan
South AfricaSince 2019, Real411 lets any citizen report a dubious post, whatever the platform; it was launched with the electoral commission and assesses content against the country’s laws and constitution.Source: Daily Maverick
IndonesiaMafindo, born from a Facebook group of ordinary citizens in 2016, relies on thousands of volunteers who check hoaxes, train others and run media literacy workshops across the country.Source: Nieman Lab
TaiwanCofacts, a citizen bot in the LINE messaging app: forward a dubious message, volunteers answer. Over 2,000 contributors. Motto: humour over rumour.Source: Taiwan Insight
UkraineLearn to Discern trained ordinary citizens in libraries and community centres to analyse the news. Participants were better at spotting disinformation than a comparison group, and still were over a year later.Source: Journal of Media Literacy Education
FinlandMedia education from early childhood, a national policy since 2013. First in Europe’s media literacy index every year since 2017.Source: Fortune
Germany and beyond“Deutschland spricht” pairs strangers with opposite views for a face-to-face talk; the format has since reached people in over 100 countries. A single conversation reduces hostility; among like-minded people, views harden.Source: My Country Talks
