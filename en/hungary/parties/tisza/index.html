The Országgyűlés elected on 12 April 2026 sits for four years, and the President of the Republic sets the date of the next vote, normally in April. Under the 17th amendment to the Fundamental Law (July 2026), anyone who has already been an MP for 12 years can no longer stand. HVG counted 31 sitting MPs affected: 22 from Fidesz, 6 from KDNP and 3 from Mi Hazánk. No Tisza MPs are affected, because none of them were in parliament before 2026. source

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

Half-wayThe new constitution has not been started: in October 2026 the Prime Minister's Office said the process would begin in the autumn, with consultations open to opposition parties and a closing referendum. Meanwhile, on 13 July 2026, it amended the current Fundamental Law alone, 139 to 6, ending the President's term, retiring constitutional judges aged 70 and over and capping MPs at 12 years. Fidesz-KDNP boycotted the vote and Mi Hazánk voted against. source · 2 · 3
Also worth knowing
  • On the eve of the election, Magyar said that even with two thirds Tisza would not vote through a one-party constitution: a new text would be drafted with others and put to a referendum, a process the government says starts in autumn 2026. In July 2026 it passed the 17th amendment to the current Fundamental Law with its own votes (139 to 6), ending the term of the President, whom Magyar counted among the 'Orbán puppets' who would not keep their posts. Fidesz-KDNP boycotted the vote and Mi Hazánk voted against. Amnesty, the Helsinki Committee, TASZ and HRW said the removals lacked procedural safeguards. source
  • Tisza attacked the outgoing government's spending, and its first budget amendment raised the 2026 deficit target from 3.7% to 7.5% of GDP. The Finance Ministry blames unfunded pre-election spending and says its review found 8.3% without action. The European Commission's May 2026 forecast had already put the 2026 deficit at 6.2%. source
  • Its programme promised independent leadership for public media. In July interim managers took over public media and news was suspended. On 20 August news resumed, mostly with former staff, and the promised open competition for a new head had not been held (Telex, August 2026). source
To be fair
  • EU finance ministers unanimously approved its €10 billion recovery plan (about €6.5 billion in grants) in July 2026, after nearly four years of freeze.
  • The European Commission approved Hungary's accession to the European Public Prosecutor's Office in July 2026.
  • It passed a new asset-declaration law and created an Asset Recovery Office, and its 17th amendment limits MPs to 12 years in office and narrows the list of two-thirds laws.

3 to 4 concrete promises from its latest programme, each checked against an official act or an independent tracker. “Made a difference” needs an independent measure of the effect on people; a promise adopted without measurable effect, symbolic or quickly reversed counts as “on paper” (a quarter of a point). “Partly” also covers a promise the party pushed but others blocked. A promise that needed power the party never held cannot be tested.

If you're considering voting for Tisza, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

On the eve of the election, Péter Magyar promised that Tisza would not vote through a one-party constitution. Three months later it amended the existing one on its own, 139 to 6, ending the President's term: not a new constitution, strictly speaking. Work on a new one, with others and a referendum, is due to start this autumn.

It promised independent public media. News was suspended in July and came back in August under interim managers, before any open competition for a new head.

Its first budget amendment raised the 2026 deficit target from 3.7% to 7.5% of GDP, which it blames on pre-election spending; the European Commission had already forecast 6.2% in May.

To be fair, EU ministers approved €10 billion of recovery funds, frozen for nearly four years, within three months of its arrival.
