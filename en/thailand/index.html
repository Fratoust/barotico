Thailand

Constitutional monarchy with a parliamentary system · 52.9 million registered voters (2026)

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. 3 referendums are needed to replace the constitution. After the first, in February 2026, the body that is supposed to write it does not exist yet.

A 2025 Constitutional Court ruling requires one referendum to start, one to approve the drafting process and one on the final text. On 8 February 2026, 59.8% voted to draft a new constitution; Thai PBS Policy Watch's projected timeline puts a new text around April–May 2029.

Sources: ISEAS Fulcrum: three referendums required (1 April 2026) · International IDEA ConstitutionNet: the 2026 referendum · Thai PBS Policy Watch: drafting timeline to 2029

Who decides what

NationalThe King is head of state. The House of Representatives (500 MPs) elects the Prime Minister by open roll-call vote; the Senate has 200 members who are not directly elected by voters.
Courts and watchdogsThe nine-judge Constitutional Court can end a prime minister’s term, as it did in August 2025. The Election Commission organises votes and can refer candidates, including senators, to the Supreme Court.
Provinces (77)Each of the 77 provinces elects constituency MPs; the provinces are also the basic unit in every draft for a constitution-drafting assembly.

Sources: Thai PBS: the House elects the Prime Minister (Section 159) · Thairath: Constitutional Court case 18/2568 · Daily News: 200 senators, 77 provinces · Thai PBS: Election Commission decision in the Senate case

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from the cabinet, MPs or citizens: in May 2026 the House took up 34 bills, 21 from the cabinet, 12 from MPs and 1 from the public.
  2. A constitutional amendment needs a majority of Parliament plus at least one third of senators (67 of 200) at the first and third readings.
  3. Replacing the constitution also needs referendums: the Constitutional Court requires three.
  4. Laws and royal decrees take effect once published in the Royal Gazette.

Sources: Thairath: 34 bills put to the House (May 2026) · ISEAS Fulcrum: amendment rules · Daily News: the one-third Senate threshold · Thairath: Royal Gazette publication of the 2025 dissolution decree

Political and economic context

Bhumjaithai (191 seats) governs with Pheu Thai (74) and smaller parties, about 292 of 500 seats. The People’s Party, first in the list vote, leads the opposition with 120 seats; Kla Tham (58), which backed Anutin in 2025, was left out and has since left the opposition too. In October 2026 the opposition filed a censure motion against 18 ministers. Any new constitution still needs 67 senators, and an Election Commission case has sent 26 sitting senators to the Supreme Court for prosecution.

Sources: Thai PBS: final seat count 2026 · PPTV: who joined the 2026 coalition · Thairath: PM vote of 19 March 2026 · ISEAS Fulcrum: Senate veto and outlook · Thairath: censure motion against 18 ministers; Kla Tham out of the opposition (October 2026)

Why it's like thisThe 2017 constitution was drafted after the 2014 coup and, according to the scholars cited here, designed to preserve the post-coup order: a Senate veto over amendments and strong courts. The Constitutional Court has dissolved parties, including Move Forward, the People’s Party’s predecessor, and has removed prime ministers.

Sources: ISEAS Fulcrum: the post-coup 2017 constitution · International IDEA ConstitutionNet: court rulings and the dissolved Move Forward Party

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Seats come from the Election Commission’s certified results as published by Thai PBS. Official party-list vote totals per party were not found in a validated source, so the page uses the unofficial count at 94% of ballots. No approval rating for the government was found: NIDA Poll and Suan Dusit Poll measure preferred prime minister and expectations instead. No independent promise tracker exists.

The homicide rate is our calculation: murder cases recorded by the Royal Thai Police (October 2022 to September 2023) divided by about 66 million inhabitants. These are cases, not victims; the UN series stops in 2011. Left-right positions are editorial estimates, as no expert survey (CHES) covers Thailand; no party reaches the far-left or far-right threshold.

Cards cover parties with at least 5 seats or 3% of the list vote. Thai Ruam Phalang (6 seats), Prachachat (5) and Palang Pracharath (5) are left out for lack of validated sources. Programme links could not be verified; promises come from the costings parties filed with the Election Commission. Income breakdowns by source are not published, so party finance shows declared donations only.

Three judicial records (Kla Tham, Democrats, Setthakij) are empty: no official act was found within the scope and with a validated source, which reflects research limits, not a finding. Press sources are limited to the outlets listed in the Media tab.
