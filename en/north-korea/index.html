North Korea

One-party state led by the Workers' Party of Korea · 687-seat assembly elected unopposed in March 2026

  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.

no free national vote≈ 1 monthsNo free national vote: the next formal national step is the Workers' Party Central Committee's year-end plenary meeting, expected in late December 2026 as in 2024 and 2025
No national office is filled by a competitive vote: Freedom House gives North Korea 0 out of 40 for political rights. Power is decided inside the Workers' Party: its 9th Congress (19-25 February 2026) re-elected Kim Jong Un General Secretary, and the Supreme People's Assembly, elected on 15 March 2026 with one pre-approved candidate per seat, re-elected him President of State Affairs on 22-23 March. Between congresses the Central Committee meets in plenary sessions; the 9th Congress itself was announced at the December 2024 plenum and prepared at the December 2025 one. No date has been announced for the next plenum, so the countdown uses late December 2026 as an estimate. The next Party Congress is due around 2031; the assembly's term was five years, and analysts believe the 2026 revision may have lengthened it. source

687 seats, 687 candidates, 687 deputies elected.

In the Supreme People's Assembly election of 15 March 2026, each constituency had one pre-approved candidate, whom voters could approve or reject, and all 687 were elected (International IDEA). The official returns give a turnout of 99.99%, with 99.93% voting for and 0.07% against (KCNA). The election itself came two years late: the assembly's term is five years, and the previous vote was in 2019 (38 North).

Sources: International IDEA: the March 2026 SPA election · KCNA: Central Election Committee press release on the returns (March 2026) · 38 North: post-congress elections, two years overdue

Who decides what

Workers' Party of KoreaThe Party leads the state. Its 9th Congress (19-25 February 2026, about 5,000 delegates) re-elected Kim Jong Un General Secretary and elected a Central Committee of 250 members (139 full, 111 alternate), 64% of them new. The Politburo Presidium has five members: Kim Jong Un, Pak Thae Song, Jo Yong Won, Kim Jae Ryong and Ri Il Hwan.
President of State Affairs and State Affairs CommissionKim Jong Un was elected President of State Affairs for the third time in March 2026, after 2016 and 2021. Under the 2026 constitution he holds command of the nuclear forces (Art. 89), serves a fixed five-year term that the assembly can no longer cut short (Art. 87), and may appoint or dismiss the assembly's chairman and the Premier between sessions (Art. 90).
Supreme People's Assembly (SPA)687 deputies, elected for five years; 108 are women (15.7%). The full assembly holds sessions; its Standing Committee amends laws between sessions. Jo Yong Won was elected its chairman in March 2026; on 6 November 2026 SBS reported the post as currently vacant; no official announcement of his departure was found.
CabinetThe Cabinet, led by Premier Pak Thae Song, runs the economy under the 'cabinet-responsibility system'; former Premier Kim Tok Hun became first vice-premier in March 2026. Analysts note that the system lets the leader keep his distance from economic results.

Sources: Asan Institute: results of the 9th Party Congress (March 2026) · 38 North: opening session of the 15th SPA · 38 North: the 2026 constitution and the State Affairs Commission · IPU Parline: Supreme People's Assembly

How a law is passed

  1. Policy lines are set by the Party Congress and Central Committee plenums; analysts expect the assembly to formalise the legal changes they decide.
  2. Between sessions, the SPA Standing Committee adopts amendments after review by the SPA Legislation Committee: on 5 November 2026 it amended the Construction Law and the Precious Metals Management Law.
  3. The full assembly amends the constitution: the March 2026 revision was not published at the time; South Korea's Ministry of Unification released its content on 6 May 2026.
  4. There is no constitutional court; between sessions the President of State Affairs may appoint, dismiss or suspend senior officials, including the assembly's chairman and the Premier (Art. 90).

Sources: SBS: SPA Standing Committee plenary meeting, 5 November 2026 · Asan Institute: the new constitution (May 2026) · 38 North: the SPA after the Party Congress

Political and economic context

North Korea has no free election, and this page cannot rank parties by votes. Freedom House scores it 3 out of 100 and describes a one-party state led by a dynastic dictatorship, with political prison camps and pervasive surveillance. Two small 'satellite' parties send deputies to the assembly, but analysts at 38 North note that both are technically subordinate to a former department of the Workers' Party; they are summarised here in one card. In 2014 the UN Commission of Inquiry found that the abuses amounted to crimes against humanity. In September 2025 the UN Human Rights Office called the decade since a 'lost decade': six laws adopted since 2015 allow the death penalty, with credible evidence of executions for distributing foreign media, alongside new fair-trial laws and two treaty ratifications. Since the UN Panel of Experts was disbanded in April 2024 after a Russian veto, eleven states monitor sanctions together; their first report (May 2025) found that over 11,000 troops and at least 100 ballistic missiles were sent to Russia in 2024. Official media are cited on this page only for what the authorities announced.

Sources: Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026: North Korea · UN Geneva: OHCHR report on the DPRK (September 2025) · Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team: first report (May 2025) · 38 North: the two satellite parties

Why it's like thisThe state presents itself as built on the ideas of its first two leaders: until 2026 the constitution's preamble called it the 'Kim Il Sung-Kim Jong Il Constitution' and honoured their goal of independent, peaceful unification; today only 'Kimilsungism-Kimjongilism' remains as the aim of state building. Kim Jong Un has rebuilt formal institutions around his posts: Party congresses in 2016, 2021 and 2026, the State Affairs Commission he has chaired since 2016, and a 2026 constitution that gives its chairman nuclear command and a term the assembly cannot shorten. In January 2024 he called the South the 'invariable principal enemy', and in 2026 unification left the constitution. The leadership argues that nuclear weapons keep the country safe; Freedom House describes a dynastic dictatorship.

Sources: Asan Institute: the new constitution (May 2026) · KEI: 2024, redefining inter-Korean relations · 38 North: the 2026 constitution · Columbia Law School: the order to 'erase' unification · Freedom House 2026: North Korea

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

North Korea is a non-free country, and much of what this page shows elsewhere does not exist here. There is no human development index or homicide figure (UNDP calculates none, and no homicide statistics are published), no population figure from a source we could verify, no opinion poll of any kind, no party accounts, no seat or vote count by party, no Workers' Party programme text (the full 2026 congress report was not published) and no independent promise tracker.

Facts about the country come from UN bodies, the Bank of Korea's estimates and recognised research institutes. Official media (KCNA) are cited only for what the authorities themselves announced, such as election returns, which cannot be checked. Press sources are limited to the most trusted South Korean broadcasters in the Reuters Institute's 2026 survey, since no free domestic press exists.

The two satellite parties, which elect deputies but do not compete with the Workers' Party, share one card. No party is placed on the left-right axis, as in other one-party systems. Rights alerts use only findings by UN treaty bodies: the 2014 Commission of Inquiry and the UN Human Rights Office's 2025 report are cited in the facts and context, not as rights alerts, because neither is a treaty body. The judicial records are empty: no domestic court decision is published, and foreign sanctions designations are not used.
