Poland

Parliamentary republic with a directly elected president · EU member since 2004

On 30 October 2026 the longest-serving of the judges elected by the current Sejm, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, suspended Tribunal president Bogdan Święczkowski and vice-president Bartłomiej Sochański for a month, accusing the president among other things of barring judges from ruling; the Prosecutor General asked the Tribunal to lift both men's immunity. Around 1 a.m. President Nawrocki, back early from Senegal, walked into the building telling the judges they were not judges of the Tribunal; she says he pushed her, and his office reported her to prosecutors. A plenary assembly of the judges is due on 17 November 2026 to consider the immunity requests.

Who decides what

PresidentElected directly for five years, two terms at most. Karol Nawrocki, backed by PiS, won the 1 June 2025 run-off with 50.89% against Rafał Trzaskowski (KO) and took office on 6 August 2025. He can veto laws, refer them to the Constitutional Tribunal and appoints judges proposed by the National Council of the Judiciary.
Council of MinistersLed by Prime Minister Donald Tusk (KO) since December 2023 and answerable to the Sejm, which can only remove it by electing a new Prime Minister (constructive vote of no confidence).
Sejm and SenateThe Sejm (460 MPs, four years) passes laws and the budget; its Marshal (Speaker) has been Włodzimierz Czarzasty (Lewica) since November 2025, under the coalition's rotation deal. The Senate (100 senators) can amend or reject bills; the Sejm overrules it by an absolute majority.
CourtsThe Constitutional Tribunal (15 judges elected by the Sejm) reviews laws; the Supreme Court and Supreme Administrative Court top the court system. Who may legitimately sit in these bodies has been disputed since the 2015-2018 reforms. On 30 October 2026 a judge elected by the current Sejm suspended the Tribunal's president; President Nawrocki contests her authority.
Regions and municipalities16 voivodeships with elected assemblies, about 380 counties (powiaty) and about 2,477 municipalities (gminy) with directly elected mayors.

Sources: Constitution, chapter IV: Sejm and Senate (president.pl) · Constitution, chapter V: the President (sejm.gov.pl) · Gov.pl: the Council of Ministers · PKW: 2025 presidential election · TVN24: the Constitutional Tribunal crisis (1 Nov 2026)

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from MPs, the Senate, the President, the Council of Ministers or 100,000 citizens.
  2. The Sejm debates them in three readings and passes them by simple majority; the Senate then has 30 days to amend or reject them.
  3. The President signs within 21 days, vetoes or refers the law to the Constitutional Tribunal. A veto falls only with three-fifths of the votes, at least half of MPs present.
  4. Changing the Constitution takes two-thirds of the Sejm and an absolute majority of the Senate.

Sources: Constitution, art. 118: who may table bills · Constitution, art. 122: signature, veto and referral by the President · Constitution, chapter IV: Sejm and Senate · Sejm: act on citizens' legislative initiative

Political and economic context

Poland has a government and a President from opposing camps. Donald Tusk's coalition governs with about 239 of 460 MPs, far from the 276 needed to override Karol Nawrocki's vetoes: on 15 October 2026 alone he vetoed three laws, one of them on carrying out European Court of Human Rights judgments, and on 13 November the bill implementing the EU Digital Services Act. The rule-of-law dispute has reached the Constitutional Tribunal itself: on 30 October 2026 a judge elected by the current Sejm suspended its president, and the President of the Republic went to the building that night to contest it. The public broadcasters have been 'in liquidation' since December 2023, which the regulator KRRiT calls a sham. Prosecutors are pursuing members of the previous government: in July 2026 Poland asked the United States to extradite former justice minister Zbigniew Ziobro over the Justice Fund, and his former deputy Marcin Romanowski is under a European arrest warrant (both deny wrongdoing; presumption of innocence applies). The right has split: in July 2026 former Prime Minister Mateusz Morawiecki took about 40 MPs out of PiS into his own club, Rozwój Plus, and Grzegorz Braun, removed from Konfederacja in January 2025, won 6.34% in the 2025 presidential first round; his Konfederacja Korony Polskiej polled 11.6% in November 2026. Meanwhile the deficit reached 7.2% of GDP in 2025.

Why it's like thisThe 1989 Round Table negotiated the end of communist rule, and the 1997 Constitution spread power to prevent its return: a government answerable to the Sejm, a directly elected President with a strong veto, a Constitutional Tribunal and a council that proposes judges. Between 2015 and 2023, PiS governments changed the Tribunal, the National Council of the Judiciary and the Supreme Court; the European Commission opened its Article 7 rule-of-law procedure in December 2017 announced on 6 May 2024 that there was no longer a clear risk of a serious breach, and closed it on 29 May. The veto meant as a safeguard is now the main brake on the government.

Left-right positions are our estimate of the Chapel Hill Expert Survey 2024, which could not be opened; Rozwój Plus and Centrum are too new to be in it. PiS and Razem are labelled far right and far left because The PopuList classifies them so. Rozwój Plus is an association and Centrum a club: neither has a programme or audited accounts yet, and party finance is broken down only where we opened the PKW resolution (PiS, PSL).

Press links come only from the validated list in the media panel: the news agency PAP, the EFCSN fact-checker Demagog and the most trusted broadcasters (Polsat News, RMF FM, TVN24). The 2026 Reuters brand-trust table could not be read, so its 2023 figures are used. Claims that only other outlets reported were rewritten from official sources or dropped, among them a count of presidential vetoes, a newspaper's tally of the '100 concretes' and one 2025 first-instance conviction.

No independent promise tally could be opened, so the tracker shows none. The homicide rate comes from a data site republishing UNODC figures. Cases are listed only with an official act (charge, indictment, ruling, immunity vote); allegations alone are left out.
