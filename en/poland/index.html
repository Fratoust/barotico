Poland

Parliamentary republic with a directly elected president · EU member since 2004

How to vote, in practice

Citizens aged 18 and over are registered automatically at their permanent address. To vote elsewhere in Poland you can ask to be added to the register where you are staying (also online, via gov.pl) or obtain a certificate to vote at any polling station. Poles abroad register with a consulate before each election. Voters with disabilities can vote by post or by proxy.

Last turnout: 74.38% in the October 2023 Sejm election, the highest since 1989; 71.63% in the June 2025 presidential run-off source

Who decides what

PresidentElected directly for five years, two terms at most. Karol Nawrocki, backed by PiS, won the 1 June 2025 run-off with 50.89% against Rafał Trzaskowski (KO) and took office on 6 August 2025. He can veto laws, refer them to the Constitutional Tribunal and appoints judges proposed by the National Council of the Judiciary.
Council of MinistersLed by Prime Minister Donald Tusk (KO) since December 2023 and answerable to the Sejm, which can only remove it by electing a new Prime Minister (constructive vote of no confidence).
Sejm and SenateThe Sejm (460 MPs, four years) passes laws and the budget; its Marshal (Speaker) has been Włodzimierz Czarzasty (Lewica) since November 2025, under the coalition's rotation deal. The Senate (100 senators) can amend or reject bills; the Sejm overrules it by an absolute majority.
CourtsThe Constitutional Tribunal (15 judges elected by the Sejm) reviews laws; the Supreme Court and Supreme Administrative Court top the court system. Who may legitimately sit in these bodies has been disputed since the 2015-2018 reforms: in 2026 four of six new Tribunal judges elected by the Sejm were not admitted to rule.
Regions and municipalities16 voivodeships with elected assemblies, about 380 counties (powiaty) and about 2,477 municipalities (gminy) with directly elected mayors.

How people vote

  • Voting is voluntary from 18. The Sejm is elected by proportional representation (D'Hondt) in 41 constituencies, with a 5% threshold for parties and 8% for coalitions; national-minority lists are exempt.
  • The 100 senators are elected by first-past-the-post in single-member districts.
  • The President is elected in two rounds; in 2025 Nawrocki won the run-off by 369,591 votes, with 71.63% turnout.

How a law is passed

  1. Bills come from MPs, the Senate, the President, the Council of Ministers or 100,000 citizens.
  2. The Sejm debates them in three readings and passes them by simple majority; the Senate then has 30 days to amend or reject them.
  3. The President signs within 21 days, vetoes or refers the law to the Constitutional Tribunal. A veto falls only with three-fifths of the votes, at least half of MPs present.
  4. Changing the Constitution takes two-thirds of the Sejm and an absolute majority of the Senate.

Why it's complicated

Poland has a government and a President from opposing camps. Donald Tusk's coalition governs with about 239 of 460 MPs, far from the 276 needed to override Karol Nawrocki, who vetoed 41 laws in his first year. The rule-of-law repair promised in 2023 is stuck: the Constitutional Tribunal's president has not admitted four of the six judges the Sejm elected in March 2026, and the public broadcasters have been 'in liquidation' since December 2023, which the regulator KRRiT calls a sham. Prosecutors are pursuing the previous government: former justice minister Zbigniew Ziobro, wanted on 26 counts over the Justice Fund, received asylum in Hungary and has been in the United States since May 2026, and his former deputy Marcin Romanowski is under a European arrest warrant (both deny wrongdoing; presumption of innocence applies). The right has split: in July 2026 former Prime Minister Mateusz Morawiecki took about 40 MPs out of PiS into his own club, Rozwój Plus, and Grzegorz Braun, expelled from Konfederacja in 2025, won 6.34% in the presidential first round with his Konfederacja Korony Polskiej. Meanwhile the deficit reached 7.3% of GDP in 2025.

Why it's like thisThe 1989 Round Table negotiated the end of communist rule, and the 1997 Constitution spread power to prevent its return: a government answerable to the Sejm, a directly elected President with a strong veto, a Constitutional Tribunal and a council that proposes judges. Between 2015 and 2023, PiS governments changed the Tribunal, the National Council of the Judiciary and the Supreme Court; the European Commission opened its Article 7 rule-of-law procedure in December 2017 and closed it on 6 May 2024, saying there was no longer a clear risk of a serious breach. The veto meant as a safeguard is now the main brake on the government.

Who's at the wheel

Prime Minister Donald Tusk (KO) since December 2023, governing with PSL, Polska 2050 and Lewica; the Centrum club, formed from a split of Polska 2050 in February 2026, keeps the climate ministry. President Karol Nawrocki (backed by PiS) since 6 August 2025.

In October 2023 PiS came first (35.38%, 194 seats) but lost its majority; KO (157 seats), Trzecia Droga (65) and Lewica (26) together won 248 of 460 seats, with 74.38% turnout. After the 2024 Razem exit and the 2026 splits, the coalition clubs total about 239 MPs (Polsat News count, July 2026).
