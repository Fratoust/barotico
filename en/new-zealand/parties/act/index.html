New Zealand

Constitutional monarchy, parliamentary system · 5.36 million people

Before you read
  • The approach: we set what parties say against what they do, and every fact links to an official or independent source.
  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.
  • Dates: the date at the top is the profile's last edit. Each section gives its own source and, where it applies, the date of the data or of the last check; in “Watch list”, some dates are upcoming deadlines.
Next vote≈ 36 monthsNext general election: expected in late 2029 (date to be set)
The 7 November 2026 election is not final yet: the figures on this page are the preliminary count of election night. The official result, including about 399,365 special votes (some 14% of the total), is due on 27 November 2026 and the writ is returned on 3 December. Parliament's term stays at three years, since the government dropped its planned referendum on a four-year term in February 2026, so the next election is expected in late 2029; the Prime Minister sets the date. source

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

The same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico does not recommend any vote.

On the posterOn the record
On the posterTough on crime: the coalition's justice agenda
⚠ Rights alertVoted for the reinstated ban on sentenced prisoners voting, enacted in the Electoral Amendment Act passed on 17 December 2025 with the coalition's votes, which bars prisoners serving a sentence from voting (not those on remand or in home detention). On 9 November 2018 the Supreme Court (Attorney-General v Taylor) upheld the High Court's declaration that the 2010 law with the same content, a ban on all sentenced prisoners voting, is inconsistent with the right to vote (s 12 of the Bill of Rights Act). The declaration did not invalidate that law. ACT voted for the ban with its coalition partners; no statement from it on the ruling was found. source · 2 · 3
On the posterA law defining the principles of the Treaty, then a referendum
Half-wayThe 2023 coalition deals committed National and NZ First to back the bill only to select committee. After more than 300,000 written submissions (90% opposed), it was defeated 112-11 at second reading in April 2025, with ACT alone in favour; David Seymour said the Treaty's principles 'are not going away'. source · 2
On the posterLess red tape and better law-making
Half-wayIts Regulatory Standards Act passed in November 2025 though more than 98% of public submissions opposed it; it sets up a board to review laws against principles that courts cannot enforce. Its minister Brooke van Velden, meanwhile, passed the pay equity overhaul under urgency in May 2025, dropping 33 claims, saying the process had become 'muddied and unclear'. source · 2
On the posterSchool choice: bring back charter schools and let state schools convert
Half-wayCharter schools returned with funding for up to 35 state-school conversions; six state schools expressed interest in the April 2025 round, and seven new charter schools served just over 200 students. source
On the posterTough on crime: the coalition's crackdown on gangs
⚠ Rights alertIts votes helped pass the Gangs Act in September 2024. On 30 July 2026 the High Court (B v Attorney-General) declared the ban on displaying gang insignia in public inconsistent with freedom of expression (s 14 of the Bill of Rights Act) and not justified, because a narrower ban could have met its aim. The declaration leaves the law in force; Justice Minister Paul Goldsmith says it will not change. source · 2 · 3
Also worth knowing
  • Champions better law-making, yet its minister Brooke van Velden passed the pay equity overhaul under urgency in May 2025, cancelling 33 current claims covering thousands of workers, with no public submissions; she said the claims process had become 'muddied and unclear'. source
  • Its Regulatory Standards Act makes consultation the first principle of good law-making; regulatory impact statements on its own charter-schools law had noted 'a condensed legislative process with limited time to engage'. David Seymour says those policies had been consulted on before. source
  • Wants less bureaucracy; its Regulatory Standards Act (November 2025) creates a new Regulatory Standards Board to review laws, and passed though more than 98% of public submissions opposed it. source
To be fair
  • Its leader's member's bill became the End of Life Choice Act (2019), approved in the 2020 referendum
  • Reopened charter (partnership) schools from 2025
  • Set up a Ministry for Regulation to review existing rules (2024)

A sample of promises, one by one
  • Partly kept A law defining the principles of the Treaty, then a referendum (2023): introduced, but its partners backed it only to select committee; defeated 112-11 in April 2025, so no referendum source
  • Kept on paper A Regulatory Standards Act (2023): passed in November 2025; its principles cannot be enforced by courts, and no measured effect yet source
  • Kept on paper Bring back charter schools and let state schools convert (2023): funding for up to 35 conversions, but only six state schools expressed interest in the 2025 round; seven new schools served just over 200 students (April 2025) source

3 to 4 concrete promises from its latest programme, each checked against an official act or an independent tracker. “Made a difference” needs an independent measure of the effect on people; a promise adopted without measurable effect, symbolic or quickly reversed counts as “on paper” (a quarter of a point). “Partly” also covers a promise the party pushed but others blocked. A promise that needed power the party never held cannot be tested.

If you're considering voting for ACT, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

It wrote consultation into law as the first principle of good law-making. Its own minister, meanwhile, passed the pay equity overhaul under urgency and cancelled 33 claims, saying the process had become 'muddied and unclear'.

Its Treaty bill drew 300,000 submissions and 11 votes. Funding was set aside for 35 charter conversions; six schools showed interest.

Its votes helped pass the gang patch ban that the High Court declared, in July 2026, an unjustified limit on freedom of expression.

To be fair, its Regulatory Standards Act is too new for anyone to have measured what it changes, which, for a party of evidence-based rules, is at least consistent.
