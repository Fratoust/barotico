New Zealand

Constitutional monarchy, parliamentary system · 5.36 million people

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

The same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico does not recommend any vote.

123 MPs in a Parliament built for 120.

Under MMP, a party that wins more electorate seats than its party vote entitles it to keeps them, and Parliament grows. In 2023 Te Pāti Māori won six electorates on a small party vote, creating an overhang. In October 2026 one poll put the party at 0.1% yet still projected it could win four electorates.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Enrolment for 2026 closed on 25 October; the Electoral Commission can now also update enrolment details automatically from other agencies' data. Enrolled voters receive an EasyVote card; advance voting runs from 26 October to 6 November. New Zealanders abroad can vote from 21 October, mainly by downloading and uploading their voting papers.

Last turnout: 78.2% of enrolled voters in the October 2023 general election (68% in the Māori electorates); 2,883,412 votes source

Who decides what

The Crown and the Governor-GeneralKing Charles III is head of state, represented by the Governor-General, who appoints the Prime Minister who has the confidence of the House and signs bills into law (Royal assent). The role is ceremonial in practice.
House of RepresentativesA single chamber of normally 120 MPs (123 in 2023-2026 because of overhang seats), elected for three years. It makes laws, approves the budget and can bring down the government.
Prime Minister and CabinetThe government governs as long as it keeps a majority in the House. Since MMP began in 1996, almost every government has been a coalition or a minority with support partners. Today: National, ACT and NZ First.
Regional and local councilsRegional councils (environment, public transport) and city and district councils (water, roads, planning, rubbish), elected every three years and funded mainly by property rates. Rates rose 6.2% in the year to September 2026.
Courts and the Waitangi TribunalIndependent courts, topped by the Supreme Court. The Waitangi Tribunal hears Māori claims that the Crown breached the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi; its recommendations are mostly not binding.

How people vote

  • Mixed-member proportional (MMP) since 1996. Each voter has two votes: a party vote, which decides each party's share of seats, and an electorate vote, which elects a local MP.
  • A party gets list seats if it wins 5% of the party vote or one electorate. That is why polls near 5% (Opportunity in 2026) or a single electorate can decide who governs.
  • Voters of Māori descent can choose the Māori roll, which elects seven Māori electorate MPs, or the general roll. Voting age is 18. In 2023, 78.2% of enrolled voters voted.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is introduced and debated at first reading.
  2. A select committee of MPs hears public submissions and proposes changes, usually over several months. The Treaty Principles Bill drew more than 300,000 written submissions.
  3. Second reading, detailed debate by the whole House, then third reading.
  4. The Governor-General gives Royal assent. There is no upper house to slow things down: the Legislative Council was abolished in 1950.
  5. Under 'urgency' the government can compress or skip stages, including the select committee. The pay equity overhaul passed this way in May 2025.

Why it's complicated

The preliminary count of 7 November produced no certain winner. Labour (29.4%) and National (29.2%) are level on the party vote, and the outgoing coalition holds 67 of 131 seats, one more than the 66 needed, only provisionally: about 399,365 special votes, which usually lean left, are added on 27 November. Opportunity entered Parliament with 7 seats and could hold the balance; Te Pāti Māori won five Māori electorates on 0.6% of the party vote, helping push the overhang to 11 seats. The next government inherits unemployment at an 11-year high (5.6%) and inflation at 3.7%, above the Reserve Bank's 1-3% target.

Why it's like thisNew Zealand has no single written constitution: its rules come from ordinary laws, conventions and the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi between the Crown and Māori chiefs, whose meaning is still debated. Separate Māori seats date from 1867. The upper house was abolished in 1950, leaving one of the most centralised parliaments in the democratic world. After first-past-the-post twice gave power to the party with fewer votes (1978 and 1981), a royal commission recommended MMP, and voters adopted it in the 1993 referendum.

Who's at the wheel

Caretaker government. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon (National), in office since November 2023, leads the outgoing National-ACT-NZ First coalition in a caretaker capacity until a new government is appointed: day-to-day business continues, while significant decisions are deferred where possible. David Seymour (ACT) has been Deputy Prime Minister since mid-term, as agreed in 2023. Nicola Willis lost her place in Parliament (National won no list seats) but stays Finance Minister under the caretaker rules.

Preliminary count, 7 November 2026 (official result 27 November): National 42, ACT 13 and NZ First 12 give the outgoing coalition 67 of 131 seats, against 66 needed; Labour 36, the Greens 16 and Te Pāti Māori 5 make 57, and Opportunity has 7. Negotiations: Luxon, who had ruled out working with Opportunity during the campaign, met its leader Qiulae Wong on 9 November and called the talk 'very positive and constructive'; she met Labour's Chris Hipkins the next day and says she will talk to National first. ACT's David Seymour says National keeping the finance portfolio is 'no longer tenable'. In 2023 the coalition was formed in six weeks, with two agreements signed on 24 November.
