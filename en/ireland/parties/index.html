Before you read
  • The approach: we set what parties say against what they do, and every fact links to an official or independent source.
  • Neutrality: the same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico recommends no vote.
  • A summary, not the whole story: read the programmes, follow several media, and make up your own mind.
  • Dates: the date at the top is the profile's last edit. Each section gives its own source and, where it applies, the date of the data or of the last check.

The Dáil elected on 29 November 2024 first met in December 2024 and can last at most five years; the election must follow within 30 days of dissolution. The Taoiseach can call it earlier. Under the coalition deal, Simon Harris (Fine Gael) is due to replace Micheál Martin (Fianna Fáil) as Taoiseach in November 2027 without an election. source
How to read this country profile

The profile has seven tabs. Start with the system to understand the rules of the game, then look at who governs and what the parties do. Every piece of information links to its source: click to check.

  1. How the country is run: institutions, elections, who passes the laws, and the political and economic context of the moment.
  2. Who is in power and how they got there, what they promised, what independent promise trackers say, and the measured effects of their action.
  3. One card per party, opened with a click: its place on the left-right axis, what it says set against what it does, its promises one by one and its democracy barometer.
  4. How to vote in practice, the electoral system, turnout, and what a blank vote changes.
  5. The country's indicators (life expectancy, schooling, homicides, press freedom, freedoms, corruption), each with its source.
  6. The country's main media, who owns them, and what to know about its news landscape.

Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

The same rules apply to every party, left or right, in government or in opposition. Barotico does not recommend any vote.

Parents on 50-hour packages may still pay around €640, and the roughly 7% of providers outside the core-funding scheme are not covered. €200 is still a target. source · 2 · 3
Also worth knowing
  • It holds the Housing ministry and promised steady progress: 2025 completions set a record (36,284) but missed the 41,000 target by 4,716, and annual targets were then dropped in favour of a 300,000-by-2030 goal. source
  • Its ard-fheiseanna long defended the UN ‘triple lock’ on troop deployments; in 2026 its government moved to abolish it, saying permanent Security Council members such as Russia, China and the US otherwise hold an effective veto. A dozen or more of its own TDs wrote that this was a ‘fundamental change’ in party policy; its whip replied that the manifesto and the programme for government had promised ‘reform’. source
  • It chose Jim Gavin as its presidential candidate; he withdrew mid-campaign in October 2025 (his name stayed on the ballot and still drew 7.2%), and Micheál Martin’s approval fell 11 points to 33%. source
To be fair
  • Topped the 2024 election with 48 seats and 21.9% of first preferences, returning Micheál Martin as Taoiseach.
  • Its housing ministers presided over the highest annual completions since the CSO series began (36,284 in 2025).
  • Led the coalition through Sinn Féin’s April 2026 no-confidence challenge, which the Government won 92–78.

If you're considering voting for Fianna Fáil, it's worth looking beyond the slogans.

It promises careful management of the public finances. Its coalition does run surpluses, yet the Fiscal Council calls its plans imprudent: the fastest growth in net spending in the EU, and €6.8 billion of overruns since Budget 2024. The Government points to the infrastructure deficit.

Its Housing ministers aimed for 41,000 homes in 2025. They delivered a record 36,284, and the annual targets were then retired in favour of 300,000 by 2030.

Its ard-fheiseanna long defended the UN 'triple lock' on troop deployments. In October 2026 the Dáil backed its government's bill to scrap it, 79 to 71; the manifesto, its whip points out, had promised 'reform'.

To be fair, those 36,284 completions were up 20.4% on 2024, the most since the CSO series began in 2011.

Half-wayIt held Housing in the Fine Gael-led government backed by Fianna Fáil (2016-20). Also worth knowing
  • It sells itself as the guardian of sound finances; the Fiscal Council says governments in which it ran Finance or Public Expenditure budgeted about 6% annual spending growth but delivered about 10%, roughly double the sustainable 5%. source

  • It signed a programme committed to Ireland’s legally binding climate targets; Budget 2027, presented by its Finance Minister Simon Harris, cut fuel levies and the carbon tax on kerosene (cuts to be phased out from spring), while the EPA projects only up to a 25% emissions cut by 2030 against a 51% target. source

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Eight parties have a card: every party with at least 3% of first preferences or a Dáil group of its own after the November 2024 election. People Before Profit–Solidarity (2.8%, 3 TDs) and 100% Redress (1 TD) appear only in the seat chart, as do the 15 independents. The Greens are kept at 3.0%, a borderline figure from an unofficial results database, which is also the source of the smaller parties' vote shares.

Seats are the official 2024 results adjusted for the May 2026 by-elections; the seven independents shown as backing the Government are the nine who signed up in January 2025 minus the two Healy-Raes, who voted against it in April 2026. No official seat count by party could be opened.

Left-right positions are our estimates, as no Chapel Hill 2024 figures were found. The homicide rate is our calculation (recorded homicide incidents divided by the CSO population). Income breakdowns are shown only for Sinn Féin and Aontú; for the others, SIPO publishes compliance reports but no breakdown we could find.

Press links come only from the validated list (RTÉ, The Irish Times, the Irish Independent, TheJournal and the Irish Examiner with regional press), chosen by trust scores in the Reuters Institute's 2026 report.
