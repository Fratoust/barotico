Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. 11 counts to fill a single Dáil seat in Galway West (May 2026).

Seventeen candidates, one seat. Under the single transferable vote, the weakest candidate is eliminated at each count and their ballots move to the next preference. The first-count leader (Independent Ireland’s Noel Thomas) was overtaken by Fine Gael’s Seán Kyne on the eleventh and final count. Patience is a civic virtue.

Who decides what

President (Uachtarán)Elected directly for 7 years (re-eligible once). Appoints the Taoiseach named by the Dáil, signs every law and, after consulting the Council of State, can refer a bill to the Supreme Court. Catherine Connolly since 11 November 2025.
Oireachtas and GovernmentDáil Éireann (174 TDs) nominates the Taoiseach, and the Government answers to it; the Seanad (60 senators: 11 named by the Taoiseach, 6 elected by university graduates, 43 from vocational panels) revises bills and can delay them.
Local government31 local authorities (26 county, 3 city and 2 city-and-county councils) in charge of housing, planning, roads, fire services and the electoral register; since June 2024 Limerick is the first to have a directly elected mayor with executive powers.
European Union14 MEPs, elected in three constituencies (Dublin, South, Midlands-North-West); EU law takes precedence in the fields the EU covers.

Sources: President of Ireland: constitutional role · Constitution of Ireland (Art. 12, 13, 18, 23, 26, 28A), Irish Statute Book · Presidential Returning Officer: 2025 presidential election · gov.ie: local government structure and functions (31 local authorities) · Limerick City and County Council: the directly elected mayor · European Parliament: how to vote in Ireland (14 MEPs)

How a law is passed

  1. A bill, usually from the Government, is published and introduced in the Dáil or the Seanad.
  2. Second stage (debate on principle), committee stage (line by line), then report and final stages.
  3. The other House repeats the process; the Seanad can amend or delay a Dáil bill (about 90 days) but cannot block it.
  4. The President signs it into law, or — after consulting the Council of State — refers it to the Supreme Court to test its constitutionality.
  5. Any change to the Constitution needs a referendum — voters have said no as well as yes (two family and care amendments were rejected in 2024).

Sources: Revenue: the legislative process (stages of a bill) · Constitution of Ireland (Art. 23, 25, 26), Irish Statute Book · President of Ireland: signing and referral of bills · RTÉ: history of Ireland's referendums (2024 family and care amendments rejected)

Political and economic context

Ireland is rich on paper: corporate tax from a few multinationals fills the coffers, yet the Fiscal Council puts the underlying deficit without that windfall at over €11bn in 2026. Record home completions have not stopped homelessness reaching 17,548 people (April 2026). Polls are fragmented, the coalition leans on regional independents, and 2026 brought fuel protests, a no-confidence motion, a narrow Dáil vote to scrap the UN ‘triple lock’ on troop deployments and a public-sector strike, followed in November by a draft pay deal worth €5.2bn that union members still have to ballot on.

Sources: Irish Fiscal Advisory Council: Fiscal Assessment Report, June 2026 · CSO: new dwelling completions, Q4 2025 · Irish Examiner: record 17,548 people in emergency accommodation (Department of Housing figures) · The Irish Times: Government wins confidence vote as Michael Healy-Rae resigns (April 2026) · The Irish Times: triple lock bill passes Dáil vote (21 October 2026) · Irish Examiner: strike called off as Government and unions agree draft pay deal (11 November 2026)

Why it's like thisIndependence in 1922 was followed by a civil war over the Treaty with Britain; Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael grew out of its two sides and are still the two big parties a century later, governing together only since 2020. The 1937 Constitution (Bunreacht na hÉireann) lets the President test bills before the Supreme Court and requires a referendum for every change. It also writes STV in multi-seat constituencies into the text: voters twice refused to change the voting system, by 51.8% in 1959 and 60.8% in 1968.

Sources: The Irish Times: coalition agreement ends almost a century of Civil War politics (June 2020) · Constitution of Ireland (Art. 16, 26), Irish Statute Book · Referendum Returning Officer: previous referendums (voting system, 1959 and 1968) · RTÉ: history of Ireland's referendums (1959: 51.8% no; 1968: 60.8% no)

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Eight parties have a card: every party with at least 3% of first preferences or a Dáil group of its own after the November 2024 election. People Before Profit–Solidarity (2.8%, 3 TDs) and 100% Redress (1 TD) appear only in the seat chart, as do the 15 independents. The Greens are kept at 3.0%, a borderline figure from an unofficial results database, which is also the source of the smaller parties' vote shares.

Seats are the official 2024 results adjusted for the May 2026 by-elections; the seven independents shown as backing the Government are the nine who signed up in January 2025 minus the two Healy-Raes, who voted against it in April 2026. No official seat count by party could be opened.

Left-right positions are our estimates, as no Chapel Hill 2024 figures were found. The homicide rate is our calculation (recorded homicide incidents divided by the CSO population). Income breakdowns are shown only for Sinn Féin and Aontú; for the others, SIPO publishes compliance reports but no breakdown we could find.

Press links come only from the validated list (RTÉ, The Irish Times, the Irish Independent, TheJournal and the Irish Examiner with regional press), chosen by trust scores in the Reuters Institute's 2026 report. No independent line-by-line tracker of the 2025 programme exists, so Fiscal Council and CSO figures are used instead. Claims without an official act or a party-level record (such as a party's vote in a confidence motion) were left out.
