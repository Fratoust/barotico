Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Check or update your registration at voter.ie (run by local councils). Applications must reach the council 15 days before polling day, not counting Sundays and public holidays. You vote in the constituency where you are registered.

Last turnout: 45.8% in the October 2025 presidential election, with a record 213,738 spoiled ballots (12.9%) source

Who decides what

President (Uachtarán)Elected directly for 7 years (max. two terms). Mostly ceremonial, but signs every law and can refer a bill to the Supreme Court. Catherine Connolly since 11 November 2025.
Oireachtas and GovernmentDáil Éireann (174 TDs) elects the Taoiseach and holds the real power; the Seanad (60 senators, mostly chosen indirectly) revises and can delay laws.
Local government31 city and county councils with limited powers (housing, planning, roads), plus a directly elected mayor in Limerick since 2024.
European Union14 MEPs; EU law takes precedence in its fields, and EU tax rules shape Ireland’s corporate tax model.

How people vote

  • Single transferable vote (STV): each constituency elects 3 to 5 TDs (174 in all). You number the candidates 1, 2, 3… in order of preference, as far down as you like.
  • A quota is set (valid votes ÷ (seats + 1), plus one). Anyone above it is elected and their surplus passes to next preferences; if nobody reaches it, the last-placed candidate is eliminated and their votes move on. Repeat until all seats are filled — so a vote is rarely wasted.
  • Voters must be 18+ and ordinarily resident. Irish citizens vote in Dáil, local and European elections and referendums; British citizens in Dáil and local elections; other EU citizens in European and local elections; everyone else in local elections only.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill, usually from the Government, is published and introduced in the Dáil or the Seanad.
  2. Second stage (debate on principle), committee stage (line by line), then report and final stages.
  3. The other House repeats the process; the Seanad can amend or delay a Dáil bill (about 90 days) but cannot block it.
  4. The President signs it into law, or — after consulting the Council of State — refers it to the Supreme Court to test its constitutionality.
  5. Any change to the Constitution needs a referendum — voters have said no as well as yes (two family and care amendments were rejected in 2024).

Why it's complicated

Ireland is rich on paper: corporate tax from a few multinationals fills the coffers, yet the Fiscal Council puts the underlying deficit without that windfall at over €11bn in 2026. Record home completions have not stopped homelessness reaching 17,548 people (April 2026). Polls are fragmented, the coalition leans on regional independents, and 2026 brought fuel protests, a no-confidence motion, a narrow Dáil vote to scrap the UN ‘triple lock’ on troop deployments and a public-sector strike.

Why it's like thisIndependence in 1922 was followed by a civil war over the Treaty with Britain; its two sides became Fine Gael and Fianna Fáil, still the two big parties a century later. The 1937 Constitution (Bunreacht na hÉireann) made the President guardian of the text and required a referendum for every change. STV was adopted partly to protect minorities; voters twice refused to replace it with first-past-the-post (1959 and 1968).

Who's at the wheel

Fianna Fáil–Fine Gael coalition backed by the Regional Independent Group. Taoiseach Micheál Martin (FF); Tánaiste and Minister for Finance Simon Harris (FG), due to become Taoiseach in November 2027.

After the 29 November 2024 election, FF (48 seats) and FG (38) agreed a programme with regional independents; the Dáil elected Martin in January 2025. In April 2026 the Government survived a Sinn Féin no-confidence motion after the surprise resignation of independent Michael Healy-Rae.
