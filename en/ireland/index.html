Ireland

Parliamentary republic · 5.53 million people (CSO, April 2026)

Reading codes

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. 11 counts to fill a single Dáil seat in Galway West (May 2026).

Seventeen candidates, one seat. Under the single transferable vote, the weakest candidate is eliminated at each count and their ballots move to the next preference. The first-count leader (Independent Ireland’s Noel Thomas) was overtaken by Fine Gael’s Seán Kyne on the eleventh and final count. Patience is a civic virtue.

Who decides what

President (Uachtarán)Elected directly for 7 years (max. two terms). Mostly ceremonial, but signs every law and can refer a bill to the Supreme Court. Catherine Connolly since 11 November 2025.
Oireachtas and GovernmentDáil Éireann (174 TDs) elects the Taoiseach and holds the real power; the Seanad (60 senators, mostly chosen indirectly) revises and can delay laws.
Local government31 city and county councils with limited powers (housing, planning, roads), plus a directly elected mayor in Limerick since 2024.
European Union14 MEPs; EU law takes precedence in its fields, and EU tax rules shape Ireland’s corporate tax model.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill, usually from the Government, is published and introduced in the Dáil or the Seanad.
  2. Second stage (debate on principle), committee stage (line by line), then report and final stages.
  3. The other House repeats the process; the Seanad can amend or delay a Dáil bill (about 90 days) but cannot block it.
  4. The President signs it into law, or — after consulting the Council of State — refers it to the Supreme Court to test its constitutionality.
  5. Any change to the Constitution needs a referendum — voters have said no as well as yes (two family and care amendments were rejected in 2024).

Why it's complicated

Ireland is rich on paper: corporate tax from a few multinationals fills the coffers, yet the Fiscal Council puts the underlying deficit without that windfall at over €11bn in 2026. Record home completions have not stopped homelessness reaching 17,548 people (April 2026). Polls are fragmented, the coalition leans on regional independents, and 2026 brought fuel protests, a no-confidence motion, a narrow Dáil vote to scrap the UN ‘triple lock’ on troop deployments and a public-sector strike.

Why it's like thisIndependence in 1922 was followed by a civil war over the Treaty with Britain; its two sides became Fine Gael and Fianna Fáil, still the two big parties a century later. The 1937 Constitution (Bunreacht na hÉireann) made the President guardian of the text and required a referendum for every change. STV was adopted partly to protect minorities; voters twice refused to replace it with first-past-the-post (1959 and 1968).

42% of people in Ireland trust most news most of the time — down 9 points in a year but above the European average (36%); trust in big brands held steady, while news avoidance hit a record 47% (Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2026, Irish report by Coimisiún na Meán and DCU). source

Documented facts to know

  • Overall trust in news fell from 51% to 42% in a year, while trust in RTÉ, the Irish Times, the Irish Independent and TheJournal stayed stable (DNR 2026). source
  • After years of scandal, RTÉ is restructuring; its public funding rises from €225m (2025) to €240m (2026) and €260m (2027). source
  • RSF 2026: Ireland 7th of 180 (score 85.93, down from 86.92); defamation reform with anti-SLAPP protection was recommended but concerns remain, and Sinn Féin has been criticised for defamation actions against journalists. Ownership, long concentrated, became more plural after Denis O’Brien sold his stakes in Independent News & Media (2019) and Communicorp (2021). source
  • 47% of people say they actively avoid the news, the highest rate recorded in the Irish survey (DNR 2026). source
