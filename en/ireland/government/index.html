Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Who governs in Ireland?

Who's at the wheel

Fianna Fáil–Fine Gael coalition backed by the Regional Independent Group. Taoiseach Micheál Martin (FF); Tánaiste and Minister for Finance Simon Harris (FG), due to become Taoiseach in November 2027.

After the 29 November 2024 election, FF (48 seats) and FG (38) agreed a programme with nine independent TDs, five of whom became ministers of state; the Dáil elected Martin in January 2025. In April 2026 the Government won a confidence vote 92–78, while independent Michael Healy-Rae resigned as a minister of state and he and his brother Danny voted against it.

Sources: gov.ie: Programme for Government 2025 · The Irish Times: the independents supporting the FF-FG coalition (January 2025) · The Irish Times: Michael Healy-Rae resigns; Government wins 92-78 (April 2026) · The Irish Times: both Healy-Raes voted against the Government (April 2026) · The Irish Times: Ipsos B&A poll, February 2026

Who holds power

President: Catherine Connolly (elected as an independent). Taoiseach: Micheál Martin (Fianna Fáil). Tánaiste and Minister for Finance: Simon Harris (Fine Gael), due to become Taoiseach in November 2027 under the coalition deal.

In government: 86 absolute majority: 88

The cabinet is made up of Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael ministers. Nine independent TDs backed the coalition in January 2025 and five of them became ministers of state, a junior rank outside the cabinet; Michael Healy-Rae resigned that post in April 2026, and he and Danny Healy-Rae voted against the Government in the confidence vote it won 92–78.

The Oireachtas members page (consulted on 15 November 2026) gives 174 TDs in 43 constituencies but no count by party. Party seats are the official November 2024 results (Oireachtas results volume: Sinn Féin 39, Labour 11, Social Democrats 11, Independent Ireland 4, People Before Profit–Solidarity 3, Aontú 2, Greens 1, 100% Redress 1, 16 non-party; Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael 86 between them, 48 and 38), adjusted for the 22 May 2026 bye-elections: Fine Gael's Seán Kyne took the Galway West seat left by independent Catherine Connolly on becoming President, and the Social Democrats' Daniel Ennis the Dublin Central seat left by Fine Gael's Paschal Donohoe (Electoral Commission, RTÉ). Changes of party whip since then were not checked one by one.

The split of the 15 independents is derived: the 7 backing the Government are the nine who backed it in January 2025 (The Irish Times) minus the two Healy-Raes (April 2026). The Ceann Comhairle chairs the Dáil and votes only to break a tie. Groups without a card are placed by our judgement; independents sit in the middle of the chart for display only.

What independent promise trackers found

  • Irish Fiscal Advisory Council (Oct 2026): budgets planned about 6% annual spending growth but delivered about 10%, double its 5% ‘speed limit’. source
  • Housing: 2025 completions (36,284) missed the 41,000 target; annual targets were then dropped for a goal of 300,000 homes by 2030 (TheJournal, Jan 2026). source
  • Irish Times (Sept 2026): completions up 11% in H1 2026 and forecast above 40,000 for the year, still trailing Government targets. source
  • Social Justice Ireland (independent NGO, May 2025): qualitative review of the programme — welcomes some measures, warns of low ambition on poverty; no scorecard. source
  • Government’s own departmental progress report on programme commitments (2025) — not independent. source

The results, as measured by independent bodies

Findings from independent public research bodies. No label, no verdict: the figures speak.

  • CSO: 36,284 new homes completed in 2025, up 20.4% on 2024 and the highest since the series began in 2011. source
  • Fiscal Council (June 2026): excluding excess corporation tax, the deficit exceeds €11bn in 2026 (over 3% of national income) and is projected above €20bn by 2030; €5 of every €6 of corporation tax is earmarked for spending. source
  • Department of Housing figures: 17,548 people in emergency accommodation in the last week of April 2026, a record. source
Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

Eight parties have a card: every party with at least 3% of first preferences or a Dáil group of its own after the November 2024 election. People Before Profit–Solidarity (2.8%, 3 TDs) and 100% Redress (1 TD) appear only in the seat chart, as do the 15 independents. The Greens are kept at 3.0%, a borderline figure from an unofficial results database, which is also the source of the smaller parties' vote shares.

Seats are the official 2024 results adjusted for the May 2026 by-elections; the seven independents shown as backing the Government are the nine who signed up in January 2025 minus the two Healy-Raes, who voted against it in April 2026. No official seat count by party could be opened.

Left-right positions are our estimates, as no Chapel Hill 2024 figures were found. The homicide rate is our calculation (recorded homicide incidents divided by the CSO population). Income breakdowns are shown only for Sinn Féin and Aontú; for the others, SIPO publishes compliance reports but no breakdown we could find.

Press links come only from the validated list (RTÉ, The Irish Times, the Irish Independent, TheJournal and the Irish Examiner with regional press), chosen by trust scores in the Reuters Institute's 2026 report. No independent line-by-line tracker of the 2025 programme exists, so Fiscal Council and CSO figures are used instead. Claims without an official act or a party-level record (such as a party's vote in a confidence motion) were left out.
