Australia

Federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy · about 27 million people

A giant summary, not the whole story

Barotico condenses complex realities into a few boxes. We try to be as neutral as possible, but politics is far richer than these profiles. Read the programmes, follow several media, talk about it, and make up your own mind. If Barotico makes you want to dig deeper, it has done its job.

Before the next vote

How to vote, in practice

Enrol or update your address with the Australian Electoral Commission (online); the roll closes shortly after an election is called. Voting is compulsory, and non-voters receive a penalty notice. You can vote early in person or by post if you cannot reach a polling place. Australians abroad can vote at overseas voting centres or by post.

Last turnout: 90.7% in the May 2025 federal election (18.1 million enrolled, 98.2% of those eligible) source

Who decides what

Federal (Commonwealth) Parliament and Government150 MPs in the House of Representatives and 76 senators. The Prime Minister governs while holding a House majority. Canberra runs defence, foreign affairs, immigration, income tax, Medicare and pensions. King Charles III is head of state, represented by the Governor-General.
Six states and two territoriesEach has its own parliament and premier or chief minister: hospitals, schools, police, roads and housing supply. Victoria votes on 28 November 2026.
Local councilsCreated by state law, not by the national Constitution: local roads, rubbish, planning approvals, libraries.
High CourtFinal court of appeal and guardian of the Constitution: it can strike down federal or state laws and rules on disputes over who holds which power.

How people vote

  • House: each of the 150 seats elects one MP by preferential vote. Voters number the candidates in order; if nobody has a majority, the last candidate's ballots move to their next choice until someone passes 50%. In 2025 Labor won 94 seats with 55.2% of the two-party-preferred vote, its best since 1975.
  • Senate: each state elects 12 senators and each territory 2, by proportional single transferable vote; half the state senators are renewed at each election. That is why small parties and independents usually hold the balance there.
  • Enrolment and voting are compulsory for citizens aged 18 and over. In 2025, 98.2% of eligible people were enrolled (a record 18.1 million) and turnout was 90.7%.

How a law is passed

  1. A bill is introduced in either House; bills that raise taxes or spend money must start in the House of Representatives.
  2. Both Houses must pass the same text. The Senate can amend or reject most bills, so a government without a Senate majority must win over the Greens, the opposition or the crossbench.
  3. If the Senate twice rejects a bill, the government can call a double-dissolution election of both Houses, followed if needed by a joint sitting.
  4. The Governor-General gives royal assent. The High Court can later strike the law down if it breaches the Constitution.
  5. The Constitution itself changes only by referendum, with a national majority and a majority in at least four of the six states.

Why it's complicated

Labor won a landslide in May 2025, yet within a year the protest vote had moved to One Nation. Newspoll had One Nation on 30%, Labor 27% and the Coalition 19% in September 2026. In between: the Bondi terrorist attack on a Hanukkah celebration (15 killed, December 2025), hate speech laws, a royal commission on antisemitism, two Coalition splits, new Liberal and Nationals leaders, One Nation's first by-election win (Farrer, May 2026), a May 2026 budget that changed negative gearing and capital gains tax, and inflation back at 4.3%. Labor has a House majority but needs the Greens or the Coalition in the Senate.

Why it's like thisThe six British colonies federated in 1901 and mixed two models: a Westminster parliament where the government answers to the lower house, and a US-style Senate where every state has equal weight, to reassure the small states. The British monarch remained head of state. Preferential voting and compulsory voting were added in the early 20th century, which is why turnout stays around 90%.

Who's at the wheel

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese (Labor) since May 2022, re-elected on 3 May 2025, with Jim Chalmers as Treasurer and Penny Wong as Foreign Minister.

Labor won 94 of 150 House seats in May 2025 with 55.2% of the two-party-preferred vote. In the Senate it holds 29 of 76 seats, so it passes laws with the Greens (10) or the Coalition (27).
