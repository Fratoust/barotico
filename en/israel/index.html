Reading codes

Red: alarming. Green: reassuring. Amber: half-way. Grey: neutral or consistent.

Promise statuses

  • Delivered done, with a measured effect for people
  • Half-way partly done, diluted, or with mixed results
  • Consistent in opposition, the party voted for its promise but was outvoted
  • Contradiction the party acted against what it claims, established by at least three sources
  • Rights alert an independent authority found the measure breached fundamental rights

⚠ Democratic watch point (in red, on party cards): a programme promise that would restrict a democratic guarantee (free elections, independent courts, freedoms, equality before the law, checks and balances, personal rights) and that an independent authority has found contrary to fundamental rights. Political goals are never flagged, only the mechanism.

Who decides what

NationalThe Knesset (120 members, one chamber) votes laws and the budget and must give its confidence to the government. The prime minister leads the government. The president, elected by the Knesset, has mostly ceremonial powers but chooses which member is tasked with forming a government after each election.
Courts and watchdogsThe Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, hears petitions against the government and the Knesset. The Attorney General issues guidelines the government must follow, for instance on what a caretaker government may do. A Central Elections Committee chaired by a Supreme Court justice runs national elections.
While no government is formedThe outgoing government keeps working until a new one is sworn in (section 30(b) of Basic Law: The Government). The law sets no explicit limits; case law and the Attorney General's guidelines ask it to act with restraint, avoid senior appointments and electoral handouts, and leave war decisions to the government or security cabinet.

Sources: Knesset: the electoral system and election day · Foreign Ministry: elections FAQ (Central Elections Committee, president's role) · IDI: what a caretaker government can do (16 November 2026)

How a law is passed

  1. Government bills go through three readings in the plenum, with committee work in between.
  2. A bill tabled by an individual member first needs a preliminary reading, usually 45 days after tabling, before following the same path.
  3. There is no written constitution. Basic Laws have higher status, yet most can be amended by 61 of the 120 members, and all three readings can take place on the same day.

Sources: Knesset Rules of Procedure: private members' bills · IDI: how Basic Laws are amended

Political and economic context

No camp has a majority. The parties of the outgoing coalition won 53 seats, the four parties around Gadi Eisenkot 48, and the two Arab-led lists 19. The six lists that opposed the outgoing government hold 67 seats together, and the paths discussed range from a national unity government with Likud to a minority government supported from outside. Meanwhile Benjamin Netanyahu's government stays on as a caretaker.

Sources: IDI: final results of the 2026 elections · IDI: composition of the outgoing government · IDI: a unity government, a remedy for deadlock? · IDI: minority governments

Why it's like thisA single nationwide constituency and a low threshold give small parties a real chance, so governments are always coalitions in which a few seats can decide. And because Basic Laws can be changed by a simple majority, the rules of the game themselves are part of the political fight: the IDI counts repeated amendments to Basic Laws in recent Knessets.

Sources: IDI: frequent changes to the Basic Laws

Method and missing data What is missing from this page, why, and how the choices were made.

This page covers the State of Israel and its citizens. Conditions in Gaza and the West Bank are examined separately by the bodies cited (Freedom House states that its score does not cover them); the page records Israeli decisions about these territories only where they are part of a party's own promises or official acts.

Seats and votes come from the Central Elections Committee's certified results as published by the Israel Democracy Institute. The approval figure is the latest IDI trust survey found (June 2025); no more recent national approval rating meeting the press rule was found.

The homicide rate is the Taub Center's figure for 2023 (terrorist attacks excluded); later counts by population group are cited in the media and watch tabs. Comparisons use OECD averages for life expectancy and schooling (Israel is an OECD member), the world rate for homicides and Transparency International's Middle East and North Africa region for corruption.

Press sources are limited to the outlets listed in the Media tab (trust survey or certified fact-checking); everything else rests on official texts, courts, the Central Elections Committee, the Central Bureau of Statistics and the Israel Democracy Institute.
